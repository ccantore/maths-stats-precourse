Question 1 — reasoning, algebra and one-variable calculus

Part A — CPA-Q01-PA (M1, M2)

Method and conditions. Quantify both variables over the stated domains. To negate a universally quantified equality, change both universal quantifiers to existential quantifiers and negate the equality. A counterexample must satisfy n≥1n\ge1 and r≠1r\ne1.

The claim is

∀n∈{1,2,…}∀r∈ℝ\{1}:∑j=0nrj=1−rn1−r. \forall n\in\{1,2,\ldots\}\;\forall r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}: \quad \sum_{j=0}^{n}r^j=\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}.

Its logical negation is

∃n∈{1,2,…}∃r∈ℝ\{1}:∑j=0nrj≠1−rn1−r. \exists n\in\{1,2,\ldots\}\;\exists r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}: \quad \sum_{j=0}^{n}r^j\ne\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}.

For example, take n=1n=1 and r=2r=2. The left-hand side is 1+2=31+2=3, whereas the claimed right-hand side is

1−21−2=1. \frac{1-2}{1-2}=1.

This admissible counterexample disproves the claim. The correct identity is

∑j=0nrj=1−rn+11−r,r≠1. \sum_{j=0}^{n}r^j=\frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}, \qquad r\ne1.

Final result. The claim is false; its exponent should be n+1n+1, not nn.

Marks (4). Universal statement: 1. Logical negation: 1. Admissible counterexample with both sides evaluated: 1. Correct finite-sum identity: 1.

Common mistakes. Negating the equality without changing the quantifiers; using the excluded value r=1r=1; keeping the incorrect exponent nn in the corrected identity.

Part B — CPA-Q01-PB (M3)

Method and conditions. The polynomial is continuous on the closed interval [−2,4][-2,4], so a global maximum exists. Compare its values at every interior stationary point and both endpoints.

Differentiate and factor:

f′(x)=−3x2+6x+9=−3(x−3)(x+1). f'(x)=-3x^2+6x+9=-3(x-3)(x+1).

The stationary points in the interval are therefore x=−1x=-1 and x=3x=3. The candidate values are

x−2−134f(x)2−52720. \begin{array}{c|rrrr} x&-2&-1&3&4\\ \hline f(x)&2&-5&27&20. \end{array}

Final result. The global maximum is

max⁡x∈[−2,4]f(x)=27, \max_{x\in[-2,4]}f(x)=27,

attained at x=3x=3.

Marks (5). Correct derivative and factorisation: 1. Both stationary points: 1. Values at the two stationary points: 1. Values at both endpoints: 1. Correct global maximum and location: 1.

Question 2 — linear systems and multivariable differentiation

Part A — CPA-Q02-PA (M4)

Method and conditions. Apply each row operation to the entire augmented row. Compare the coefficient rank with the number of unknowns after elimination.

Write the augmented matrix and eliminate:

[11032−1140112]→R2←R2−2R1[11030−31−20112]. \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&0&3\\ 2&-1&1&4\\ 0&1&1&2 \end{array} \right] \xrightarrow{R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&0&3\\ 0&-3&1&-2\\ 0&1&1&2 \end{array} \right].

Next use R2←R2+3R3R_2\leftarrow R_2+3R_3:

[110300440112]. \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&0&3\\ 0&0&4&4\\ 0&1&1&2 \end{array} \right].

Thus z=1z=1, then y=1y=1, and then x=2x=2. There are three pivots in the coefficient columns, so rank⁡(𝐀)=3\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})=3. The augmented matrix also has rank 33.

Final result. The system has the unique solution

(x,y,z)=(2,1,1). (x,y,z)=(2,1,1).

Marks (4). Traceable valid elimination/row-operation evidence showing three pivots: 1. Correct solution: 1. Correct coefficient rank: 1. Unique-solution classification justified by rank and the number of unknowns: 1. Substitution-only work does not earn the first mark.

Common mistakes. Applying a row operation only to the coefficient entries; omitting the rank statement; classifying the system from the number of equations rather than its pivots.

Part B — CPA-Q02-PB (M6)

Method and conditions. The maps are polynomial or linear, so they are differentiable everywhere. Under the column-gradient convention,

∇(F∘𝐆)(𝐱)=𝐉𝐆(𝐱)𝖳∇F(𝐆(𝐱)). \nabla(F\circ\mathbf{G})(\mathbf{x}) =\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf T} \nabla F\bigl(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x})\bigr).

Here

∇F(u,v)=[2u+vu]∈ℝ2×1,𝐉𝐆(x,y)=[111−1]∈ℝ2×2. \nabla F(u,v)= \begin{bmatrix} 2u+v\\ u \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{2\times1}, \qquad \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-1 \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}.

Since

𝐆(1,0)=[11],∇F(1,1)=[31], \mathbf{G}(1,0)= \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \nabla F(1,1)= \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix},

the chain rule gives a 2×12\times1 column vector:

∇h(1,0)=𝐉𝐆(1,0)𝖳∇F(1,1)=[111−1][31]=[42]. \nabla h(1,0) =\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{G}}(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\nabla F(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix}.

For the requested direct check, first simplify the composition:

h(x,y)=(x+y)2+(x+y)(x−y)=2x2+2xy. h(x,y)=(x+y)^2+(x+y)(x-y)=2x^2+2xy.

Therefore

∂h∂x=4x+2y,∂h∂x(1,0)=4, \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}=4x+2y, \qquad \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(1,0)=4,

which agrees with the first component above.

Final result. ∇h(1,0)=[4,2]𝖳\nabla h(1,0)=[4,2]^{\mathsf T}.

Marks (4). Correct derivative objects, evaluation point and dimensions: 1. Dimensionally correct chain-rule setup: 1. Correct column gradient: 1. Correct direct verification of the first component: 1.

Common mistakes. Writing the gradient as a row; reversing the chain-rule multiplication order; evaluating ∇F\nabla F at (1,0)(1,0) instead of at 𝐆(1,0)\mathbf{G}(1,0); omitting the direct check.

Question 3 — probability rules and Bayes’ rule

Part A — CPA-Q03-PA (S1)

Method and conditions. Use inclusion–exclusion. The exactly-one event excludes the intersection, and the neither event is (A∪B)c(A\cup B)^c.

First,

ℙ(A∪B)=35+12−14=1720. \mathbb{P}(A\cup B) =\frac35+\frac12-\frac14 =\frac{17}{20}.

The probability of exactly one event is

ℙ(A∪B)−ℙ(A∩B)=1720−14=35. \mathbb{P}(A\cup B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B) =\frac{17}{20}-\frac14 =\frac35.

Finally,

ℙ((A∪B)c)=1−1720=320. \mathbb{P}\bigl((A\cup B)^c\bigr) =1-\frac{17}{20} =\frac3{20}.

Final result. The three probabilities are 17/2017/20, 3/53/5 and 3/203/20, respectively.

Marks (3). One mark for each correct probability with a valid setup.

Common mistakes. Adding the event probabilities without subtracting their overlap; including the intersection in exactly one; using the complement of A∩BA\cap B for neither.

Part B — CPA-Q03-PB (S2)

Method and conditions. The events HH and HcH^c partition the sample space. Use the law of total probability for SS, then apply Bayes’ rule. Here ℙ(Hc)=3/4\mathbb{P}(H^c)=3/4.

The two positive-screen branches are

ℙ(S∩H)=ℙ(S∣H)ℙ(H)=4514=15, \mathbb{P}(S\cap H) =\mathbb{P}(S\mid H)\mathbb{P}(H) =\frac45\frac14 =\frac15,

and

ℙ(S∩Hc)=ℙ(S∣Hc)ℙ(Hc)=1534=320. \mathbb{P}(S\cap H^c) =\mathbb{P}(S\mid H^c)\mathbb{P}(H^c) =\frac15\frac34 =\frac3{20}.

Therefore

ℙ(S)=15+320=720. \mathbb{P}(S)=\frac15+\frac3{20}=\frac7{20}.

Bayes’ rule gives

ℙ(H∣S)=ℙ(S∣H)ℙ(H)ℙ(S)=1/57/20=47. \mathbb{P}(H\mid S) =\frac{\mathbb{P}(S\mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)}{\mathbb{P}(S)} =\frac{1/5}{7/20} =\frac47.

Final result. ℙ(S)=7/20\mathbb{P}(S)=7/20 and ℙ(H∣S)=4/7\mathbb{P}(H\mid S)=4/7.

Marks (4). Correct two branch probabilities: 1. Correct total ℙ(S)\mathbb{P}(S): 1. Correct Bayes setup and direction: 1. Correct posterior: 1.

Common mistakes. Reversing the conditional probability; omitting the HcH^c branch; using ℙ(H)\mathbb{P}(H) rather than ℙ(S)\mathbb{P}(S) in the denominator.

Question 4 — random variables and moments

Part A — CPA-Q04-PA (S3)

Method and conditions. A PMF must sum to one over its stated support. The CDF includes its endpoint: FX(1)=ℙ(X≤1)F_X(1)=\mathbb{P}(X\le1).

Normalisation gives

c(1+2+3)=1, c(1+2+3)=1,

so c=1/6c=1/6. Hence

ℙ(X≥1)=pX(1)+pX(2)=26+36=56, \mathbb{P}(X\ge1)=p_X(1)+p_X(2)=\frac26+\frac36=\frac56,

and

FX(1)=pX(0)+pX(1)=16+26=12. F_X(1)=p_X(0)+p_X(1)=\frac16+\frac26=\frac12.

Final result. c=1/6c=1/6, ℙ(X≥1)=5/6\mathbb{P}(X\ge1)=5/6, and FX(1)=1/2F_X(1)=1/2.

Marks (3). Correct normalising constant: 1. Correct upper-tail probability: 1. Correct CDF value with the endpoint included: 1.

Common mistakes. Failing to normalise the PMF; starting the stated support at one; using ℙ(X<1)\mathbb{P}(X<1) for FX(1)F_X(1).

Part B — CPA-Q04-PB (S4)

Method and conditions. Use linearity of expectation and the variance formula for a linear combination. The given finite variances and covariance are sufficient.

The expectation is

𝔼[Z]=2𝔼[X]−𝔼[Y]=2(1)−2=0. \mathbb{E}[Z] =2\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[Y] =2(1)-2 =0.

The variance is

Var⁡(Z)=4Var⁡(X)+Var⁡(Y)−4Cov⁡(X,Y)=4(2)+3−4(12)=9. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(Z) &=4\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)-4\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\\ &=4(2)+3-4\left(\frac12\right)\\ &=9. \end{aligned}

Incorrectly treating XX and YY as independent would omit the covariance term and give 8+3=118+3=11. The positive covariance lowers the variance by 22 because the coefficients of XX and YY in ZZ have opposite signs.

Final result and interpretation. 𝔼[Z]=0\mathbb{E}[Z]=0 and Var⁡(Z)=9\operatorname{Var}(Z)=9; the positive covariance lowers this variance from the incorrect independence value of 1111.

Marks (3). Correct expectation: 1. Correct variance setup and value: 1. Correct comparison and sign interpretation: 1.

Common mistakes. Ignoring the covariance term; failing to square the coefficient (2); using a positive covariance contribution despite the opposite-signed coefficients.
