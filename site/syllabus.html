Economics PhD Mathematics and Statistics pre-course syllabus

Course description

The Mathematics and Statistics Pre-course is an eight-week, self-study programme for incoming PhD Economics students. It is aimed at restore or build fluency in the mathematical, probability and statistical tools expected at the start of doctoral study and prepares you for the diagnostic entry exams you will take at the start of your PhD. It is a preparation course, with the aim of identifying and addressing any weaknesses in the key mathematical, probability and statistical tools expected at the start of doctoral study in Economics.

What the course covers

The mathematics part of the course moves from notation, reasoning, algebra and one-variable calculus (M1–M3) through linear algebra (M4–M5), multivariable calculus and curvature (M6–M8), and optimisation (M9–M11).

The probability and statistics part of the course moves from probability rules and conditional probability (S1–S2) through random variables, distributions and moments (S3–S5), and then sampling, estimation and elementary statistical inference (S6–S9). The sequence ends with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

The eight-week schedule gives the exact weekly outcomes, required written readings, resources and what you should read or skip from each source weekly.

How the course works

Each normal week requires approximately 9–11 hours of self-study. This is an estimate and the time you will need will ultimately depend on your prior knowledge and experience. Each week you will:

  1. read the assigned written sections;
  2. reproduce important worked examples by hand;
  3. complete the separate weekly problem sheet when it is released;
  4. use progressive hints only after a serious attempt;
  5. mark your work from complete solutions;
  6. record errors by learning-outcome ID; and
  7. reattempt errors two or three days later.

Required work does not include videos, applets, programming or specialist software. Questions are designed to be solved by hand. A basic non-programmable scientific calculator may be permitted for specified assessments, with any necessary critical values supplied.

Assessment and feedback

The course includes two formative checkpoints and two separate timed mock papers: 90 minutes for Maths and 90 minutes for Statistics. They diagnose strengths and revision priorities by learning outcome; they are not pass/fail decisions. The mock papers are practice for the two exams you will take at the start of your PhD.

The required course and every assessment use only outcomes M1–M11 and S1–S9. Any extension material (marked as R1 and R2) is kept separate from the core course and does not contribute to the weekly core workload or any examination.

However, part of the bridge notes, examples and exercises have been created with the help of some LLM models. Therefore there might be some mistakes or inconsistencies in the material. If you find any, please let us know by emailing cristiano.cantore@uniroma1.it with the week, item or source locator. Checkpoint and mock scores are diagnostic only. They are not used for admission, progression, ranking, funding or grading. Their marking standards and profile bands are provisional for this pilot.

Weekly workload

Plan approximately 9–11 hours of core study each week. However, this will ultimately depend on your own background and learning style. A normal allocation is 4–5 hours for reading and reproducing worked examples, 4–5 hours for the weekly problem sheet, and up to one hour for a self-test, marking and error logging. The weekly allocation changes slightly when a checkpoint or one of the mock papers is due.

Course materials

Required materials are written and text-only. The course uses:

  • exact section-level external readings;
  • course notes and notation reminders;
  • worked examples;
  • weekly problem sheets;
  • progressive hints and complete solutions;
  • short self-tests;
  • formula or distribution sheets where needed; and
  • two checkpoints and two separate mock papers: Maths and Statistics.

No video, applet, programming language, statistical package or specialist software is required. Each week schedule contains specific instructions of what to read and what to skip. Follow every skip instruction in the schedule, even if the linked source offers additional chapters, exercises or media.

Study sequence

Use the same sequence each week:

  1. Check the week’s outcome IDs and retrieve the listed prerequisites.
  2. Read only the exact assigned sections and observe every skip instruction.
  3. Close the source and reproduce the important definitions, conditions and worked steps from memory.
  4. Attempt the weekly problems without opening the full solutions.
  5. Consult progressive hints only after a serious attempt.
  6. Mark the work, recording the outcome ID and cause of each error.
  7. Reattempt missed work two or three days later.
  8. Complete the self-test under closed-note conditions.

The study protocol gives a practical session plan and an error-log template.

Working conventions

Use the notation established in the course materials. In particular, vectors and gradients are columns, and the Jacobian of a function from ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m is an mm by nn matrix. State distribution parameterisations and the conditions needed for a method. For inequality constraints, use the course’s maximisation convention with constraints written gj(𝐱)≥0g_j(\mathbf{x})\ge 0, multipliers λj≥0\lambda_j\ge 0, and Lagrangian term +λjgj(𝐱)+\lambda_j g_j(\mathbf{x}).

What successful completion looks like

By the end of Week 8, you should be able to attempt both 90-minute mock papers under timed conditions, diagnose errors by outcome ID, and identify a short, specific revision list. Completion is evidence of preparation; it is not a guarantee of a particular result in either entry exam paper.

Study protocol

Purpose

Use this protocol every week. It turns a large reading list into repeated, checkable study and makes your error log useful for later revision. The normal core workload is approximately 9–11 hours per week; use the exact allocation in the eight-week schedule.

Before the week starts

  1. Write the week’s learning-outcome IDs at the top of a new page.
  2. Read the prerequisite and retrieval line in the schedule.
  3. Reserve three to five study blocks across the week rather than one long sitting.
  4. Reserve a second attempt two or three days after the main problem-solving block.
  5. Open only the exact reading sections listed in the schedule.

Required work is text-only. You do not need a video, applet, programming language, statistical package or specialist software. If a linked source offers one, follow the schedule’s skip instruction.

The standard cycle

1. Read actively

For each assigned section:

  • identify the definition, method or result attached to the stated outcome;
  • record every condition needed for the result;
  • note the dimensions, support or parameter restrictions where relevant;
  • stop at the assigned locator; and
  • obey the explicit skip instruction before following any source navigation.

Do not copy paragraphs. Write a brief original summary after closing the source.

2. Reproduce worked examples

Close the worked solution and reproduce the example by hand. Label each step with its purpose: representation, condition, transformation, calculation or interpretation. Reopen the source only after you have reached a result or a specific impasse.

If your result differs, locate the first divergent step. Record that step, not merely the final mismatch.

3. Attempt the weekly problem sheet

Start without hints or full solutions. Write conditions and conventions before calculating. If you are stuck, make one specific written statement about what is known and what remains to be found.

After a serious attempt, use progressive hints in order. Stop reading as soon as a hint gives you a next step, then return to your own work. Do not open the full solution until the attempt is complete.

4. Mark and diagnose

Mark from the complete solution. Use a different colour or margin, but do not erase the original attempt. Classify the first cause of each error:

  • CONCEPT — a definition, condition or interpretation was missing;
  • REPRESENTATION — the event, equation, matrix, function or distribution was set up incorrectly;
  • METHOD — the wrong procedure or result was selected;
  • ALGEBRA — the setup was valid but manipulation failed;
  • NOTATION — dimensions, orientation, symbols, support or parameters were inconsistent;
  • ARITHMETIC — the mathematical route was correct but calculation failed; or
  • TIME — you could complete the work accurately but not within the target.

5. Reattempt after spacing

Two or three days later, reattempt each incorrect or incomplete part from a blank page. Do not reread your first solution before starting. Close an error only when you can produce and explain a correct solution without the hint.

6. Self-test under closed-note conditions

Complete the weekly self-test without notes, sources or solutions. Use only the calculator permission stated for that artifact. Mark it immediately and add any new errors to the same log.

7. Targeted recovery without a progression pause

If your score on a current-outcome self-test is below 60%, or an item remains unresolved after its spaced reattempt, take a targeted recovery route before the next retrieval block. Use the relevant week’s prerequisite/retrieval line, reproduce the named worked example for that outcome, complete the first matching exercise part, record the result in the error log, then reattempt the original item from a blank page.

This is a study recommendation, not a hard pause. It has no effect on admission, progression, ranking, funding, grading or sanctions.

At the end of the week, add a “no error yet” entry for any current outcome that has not produced an error. This prevents an empty log from being mistaken for evidence of mastery.

When time is short

Protect the core in this order:

  1. required reading at the exact locator;
  2. one reproduced example for each current outcome;
  3. the complete weekly problem sheet;
  4. marking and diagnosis;
  5. spaced reattempt; and
  6. the self-test.

Do not replace required work with extension material. Do not compensate for a missed week by assigning yourself unlisted chapters. Carry unresolved outcome IDs into the next week’s retrieval block and use the error log to choose the minimum targeted review.

Entry-exam information

The pre-course prepares you for a diagnostic entry examination consisting of two separate papers: Maths and Statistics. The diagnosis is intended to identify preparation needs; this course does not define a pass/fail threshold.

The examination dates, locations, invigilation and final candidate instructions will be announced in due course.

Examined preparation boundary

The examined preparation boundary is exactly the required learning outcomes M1–M11 and S1–S9 in the student syllabus. The statistics boundary ends with elementary estimation, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing (S7–S9). Extension outcomes have no checkpoint, mock or diagnostic-exam mapping.

Mock structure

The mock is split into a 90-minute, 55-mark Maths paper and a separate 90-minute, 45-mark Statistics paper. Each begins with a short diagnostic section.

The Maths paper then has longer sections on:

  • linear algebra (M4–M5);
  • multivariable calculus (M6–M7);
  • curvature and optimisation (M8–M11).

The Statistics paper then has longer sections on:

  • probability, random variables and conditional distributions (S1–S5);
  • sampling and limit results (S6); and
  • estimation and inference (S7–S9).

Every required outcome has at least one explicit scorable mapping on its subject paper. Each paper score should therefore be read together with its diagnostic profile, not on its own.

Examination paper structure

The examination follows the same split: one 90-minute Maths paper and one separate 90-minute Statistics paper. The examination questions are not the mock questions.

Practice conditions

For both checkpoints and each mock paper:

  • complete the paper without notes or solutions;
  • do not use hints during the timed attempt;
  • a basic non-programmable scientific calculator is permitted;
  • programming, computer algebra and statistical software are not permitted or required;
  • use the supplied formula sheet and any supplied probability table or critical values; and
  • show the method, conditions and interpretation needed for the allocated marks.

All questions are intended to be hand-solvable. Calculator use never replaces the derivation or reasoning shown on the page.

Interpreting feedback

Use the mathematics and probability/statistics profiles separately. For each missed part:

  1. record its outcome ID;
  2. identify the first error using the categories in the study protocol;
  3. return to the corresponding week in the eight-week schedule; and
  4. reattempt the item after two or three days.

Provisional readiness descriptions in course feedback are diagnostic bands, not entry-exam pass marks. The programme, not this pre-course, determines how the two entry exam papers are used.
