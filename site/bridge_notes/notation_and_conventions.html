Notation and conventions

This card is a translation layer between the assigned readings. It fixes the notation used in this course; it does not replace the derivations in the Week 1 reading route, Week 4 reading route, Week 5 reading route or Week 7 reading route. Use := when introducing a definition and = for an asserted equality. Define domains, dimensions, supports and regularity conditions whenever they affect a calculation.

Sets, logic and functions

The real numbers are ℝ\mathbb{R}, Euclidean nn-space is ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, the natural numbers are ℕ\mathbb{N}, and the empty set is ⌀\varnothing. If whether 0∈ℕ0\in\mathbb{N} matters, state the convention; for sample sizes and finite sums below, nn is a positive integer. For sets, use a∈Aa\in A (membership), A⊆BA\subseteq B (inclusion), A∪BA\cup B (union), A∩BA\cap B (intersection) and AcA^c (complement relative to the stated universe). For propositions PP and QQ, use ∀\forall (for all), ∃\exists (there exists), P⇒QP\Rightarrow Q (implication) and P⇔QP\Leftrightarrow Q (equivalence). An implication is not an equality.

Write a function with its domain and codomain when they matter, for example f:ℝn→ℝm,𝐱↦f(𝐱). f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m,\qquad \mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}). Here nn is the number of inputs and mm the number of outputs. Intervals use (a,b)(a,b), [a,b][a,b], (a,b](a,b] or [a,b)[a,b); the brackets, not surrounding prose, state endpoint inclusion.

Logs, sums and products

The unqualified logarithm is natural: log⁡x\log x means base ee, where ee is Euler’s number, and requires x>0x>0. For another base write log⁡bx\log_b x, where b>0b>0 and b≠1b\ne1. In particular, log identities are used only where their arguments are in the logarithm’s domain.

For scalars aia_i indexed by i=1,…,ni=1,\ldots,n, write ∑i=1nai\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i and ∏i=1nai\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i. A geometric sequence with first term aa, common ratio rr, and final index N∈ℕ∪{0}N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\} has ∑t=0Nart={a1−rN+11−r,r≠1,(N+1)a,r=1. \sum_{t=0}^{N}ar^t= \begin{cases} a\dfrac{1-r^{N+1}}{1-r},&r\ne1,\\ (N+1)a,&r=1. \end{cases} The infinite version is written only after its convergence condition is checked: ∑t=0∞art=a1−rwhen |r|<1. \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}ar^t=\frac{a}{1-r} \qquad\text{when }|r|<1.

Scalars, vectors and matrices

Scalars use italic lower-case letters such as xx, aa and θ\theta. Vectors use bold lower-case letters and are always columns; thus 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n means an n×1n\times1 vector. Matrices use bold upper-case letters. If 𝐀∈ℝm×n\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, it has mm rows and nn columns. The identity matrix is 𝐈n\mathbf{I}_n, and a zero matrix is 𝟎m×n\mathbf{0}_{m\times n} when its dimensions need to be made explicit. Use 𝐀𝖳\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T} and rank⁡(𝐀)\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) for any rectangular matrix. For a square matrix 𝐀∈ℝn×n\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, use det⁡(𝐀)\det(\mathbf{A}) and tr⁡(𝐀)\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}); use 𝐀−1\mathbf{A}^{-1} only when that square matrix is nonsingular.

For 𝐱,𝐲∈ℝn\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n, the Euclidean inner product is ⟨𝐱,𝐲⟩:=𝐱𝖳𝐲\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle:= \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}. A linear system is 𝐀𝐱=𝐛\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, with 𝐀∈ℝm×n\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n and 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m. For 𝐀∈ℝn×n\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, an eigenvalue λ∈ℝ\lambda\in\mathbb{R} and corresponding eigenvector 𝐯∈ℝn\{𝟎}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\} satisfy 𝐀𝐯=λ𝐯\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}.

Differentiation and dimension checks

For a one-variable function use f′(x)f'(x), f″(x)f''(x), or df/dx\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}x. For f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, the gradient is the column ∇f(𝐱):=[∂f/∂x1⋮∂f/∂xn]∈ℝn. \nabla f(\mathbf{x}):= \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^n. For a vector-valued map 𝐟:ℝn→ℝm\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m with components f1,…,fmf_1,\ldots,f_m, the Jacobian has one output per row and one input per column: 𝐉𝐟(𝐱):=[∂fi∂xj]i=1,…,m;j=1,…,n∈ℝm×n. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}):= \left[\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\right]_{ i=1,\ldots,m;\,j=1,\ldots,n} \in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}. Thus the 1×n1\times n Jacobian of a scalar function is the transpose of its n×1n\times1 gradient. A source row such as [fx1,…,fxn][f_{x_1},\ldots,f_{x_n}] is therefore translated as ∇f=[fx1,…,fxn]𝖳\nabla f=[f_{x_1},\ldots,f_{x_n}]^{\mathsf T}.

The Hessian of ff is the n×nn\times n matrix 𝐇f(𝐱):=[∂2f∂xi∂xj]i,j=1,…,n. \mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x}):= \left[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}\right]_{i,j=1, \ldots,n}. If the second partial derivatives are continuous near the point, the mixed partials agree and this Hessian is symmetric. For a small column change d𝐱∈ℝn\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n, the total differential is df=∇f(𝐱)𝖳d𝐱,(1×n)(n×1)=1×1. \mathrm{d}f=\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf T}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}, \qquad (1\times n)(n\times1)=1\times1.

For differentiable maps 𝐠:ℝp→ℝn\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^n and 𝐟:ℝn→ℝm\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m, let 𝐡:=𝐟∘𝐠\mathbf{h}:=\mathbf{f}\circ\mathbf{g}. Then 𝐉𝐡(𝐮)=𝐉𝐟(𝐠(𝐮))𝐉𝐠(𝐮),(m×n)(n×p)=m×p. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{u}) =\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})) \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{u}), \qquad (m\times n)(n\times p)=m\times p. If the outer map is scalar-valued, the equivalent column-gradient form is ∇(f∘𝐠)(𝐮)=𝐉𝐠(𝐮)𝖳∇f(𝐠(𝐮)). \nabla(f\circ\mathbf{g})(\mathbf{u}) =\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{u})^{\mathsf T} \nabla f(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})). For a differentiable path 𝐱(t)\mathbf{x}(t) from a scalar tt, this reduces to df(𝐱(t))/dt=∇f(𝐱(t))𝖳𝐱′(t)\mathrm{d}f(\mathbf{x}(t))/\mathrm{d}t= \nabla f(\mathbf{x}(t))^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}'(t).

Suppose F(x0,θ0)=0F(x_0,\theta_0)=0, FF is continuously differentiable near (x0,θ0)(x_0,\theta_0), and Fx(x0,θ0)≠0F_x(x_0,\theta_0)\ne0. Then the equation defines a differentiable local solution x(θ)x(\theta), and along that solution dxdθ=−FθFx. \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\theta}=-\frac{F_\theta}{F_x}. For a system 𝐅:ℝn×ℝ→ℝn\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^n\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^n, suppose 𝐅(𝐱0,θ0)=𝟎\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_0,\theta_0)=\mathbf{0}, the map is continuously differentiable nearby, and the Jacobian with respect to 𝐱\mathbf{x} is nonsingular at (𝐱0,θ0)(\mathbf{x}_0,\theta_0). The system then defines a differentiable local solution 𝐱(θ)\mathbf{x}(\theta). Let 𝐉𝐅,𝐱\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{F},\mathbf{x}} be the n×nn\times n Jacobian with respect to 𝐱\mathbf{x}, and let 𝐅θ∈ℝn\mathbf{F}_\theta\in\mathbb{R}^n collect the partial derivatives with respect to θ\theta. Along the local solution, the derivative is 𝐱′(θ)=−𝐉𝐅,𝐱−1𝐅θ. \mathbf{x}'(\theta)= -\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{F},\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{F}_\theta.

If ff is twice continuously differentiable near the scalar point aa, Taylor notation records its centre: the second-order approximation about aa is f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+12f″(a)(x−a)2. f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\tfrac12f''(a)(x-a)^2.

Curvature and optimisation conventions

A quadratic form is q(𝐱):=𝐱𝖳𝐀𝐱q(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}, where 𝐀∈ℝn×n\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}. Definiteness tests use the symmetric part 𝐀s:=12(𝐀+𝐀𝖳)\mathbf{A}_{s}:=\frac12(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}). After the terms are defined, 𝐀s≻0\mathbf{A}_{s}\succ0, 𝐀s≽0\mathbf{A}_{s}\succeq0, 𝐀s≺0\mathbf{A}_{s}\prec0 and 𝐀s≼0\mathbf{A}_{s}\preceq0 mean positive definite, positive semidefinite, negative definite and negative semidefinite. Curvature conclusions are global only with the relevant domain and differentiability assumptions. Quasiconcavity means convex upper contour sets; it is not a synonym for concavity.

For a scalar maximisation problem with equality constraints hk(𝐱,𝐚)=0h_k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})=0, k=1,…,Kk=1,\ldots,K, where KK is the number of equalities and 𝐚\mathbf{a} is a parameter vector, use ℒ(𝐱,𝛍;𝐚):=f(𝐱,𝐚)+∑k=1Kμkhk(𝐱,𝐚). \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{a}) :=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}) +\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_kh_k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}). For continuously differentiable data, at a regular local optimum where the equality-constraint gradients are linearly independent, unrestricted multipliers μk\mu_k exist such that stationarity in 𝐱\mathbf{x} and all equalities hold. Solutions of these equations are candidates; concavity, feasibility and other stated assumptions are needed for a global conclusion. The sign of a multiplier is tied to the constraint’s encoding. For a value function V(𝐚)V(\mathbf{a}), the envelope derivative is ∂V∂ar=∂ℒ∂ar|* \frac{\partial V}{\partial a_r} =\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_r}\right|_* under the stated regularity and optimality conditions, where ** denotes the optimum.

The course-wide Kuhn–Tucker convention is maximise f(𝐱)f(\mathbf{x}) subject to gj(𝐱)≥0g_j(\mathbf{x})\ge0, j=1,…,Jj=1,\ldots,J, and hk(𝐱)=0h_k(\mathbf{x})=0, k=1,…,Kk=1,\ldots,K. With non-negative multipliers λj≥0\lambda_j\ge0 and unrestricted equality multipliers μk\mu_k, write ℒ(𝐱,𝛌,𝛍):=f(𝐱)+∑j=1Jλjgj(𝐱)+∑k=1Kμkhk(𝐱). \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}) :=f(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_jg_j(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_kh_k(\mathbf{x}). The four checks are stationarity ∇𝐱ℒ=𝟎\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{0}, primal feasibility gj≥0,hk=0g_j\ge0,\ h_k=0, dual feasibility λj≥0\lambda_j\ge0, and complementary slackness λjgj=0\lambda_jg_j=0. Write a restriction such as xi≥0x_i\ge0 explicitly as a constraint. Rewrite a minimisation question into this maximisation convention before applying the template. With differentiable data and a suitable constraint qualification, these conditions are necessary at a regular local optimum. In general they identify candidates, not optima; they are sufficient only when an applicable concavity result and a convex feasible set supply the required global conditions.

Probability and random variables

Use Ω\Omega for the sample space and upper-case Roman letters such as AA and BB for events. Their probabilities are ℙ(A)\mathbb{P}(A), and conditional probability is ℙ(A∣B)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B). Independence can be recorded as ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(A)ℙ(B)\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B). Random variables use upper-case letters X,YX,Y; their realised values use lower-case letters x,yx,y. Random vectors use bold upper-case letters.

For a discrete XX, write its PMF as pX(x)p_X(x); for a continuous variable, write its density as fX(x)f_X(x); in either case write the support alongside the formula. The CDF is FX(x):=ℙ(X≤x)F_X(x):=\mathbb{P}(X\le x). Use 𝔼[X]\mathbb{E}[X], Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(X), Cov⁡(X,Y)\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) and Corr⁡(X,Y)\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y). Conditional expectation is 𝔼[X∣Y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] as a random variable and 𝔼[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] at the value yy.

When conditional probability is written as a ratio, ℙ(A∣B):=ℙ(A∩B)/ℙ(B)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B):=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B) requires ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0.

Sampling and inference distinctions

A random sample from a population distribution FθF_\theta indexed by the parameter θ\theta is written X1,…,Xn∼iidFθ. X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}F_\theta. The label iid asserts both independence and identical distribution; do not use it for independent but non-identically distributed observations. For n≥2n\ge2, use X‾:=1n∑i=1nXi,S2:=1n−1∑i=1n(Xi−X‾)2. \bar X:=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i, \qquad S^2:=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)^2. After observing data 𝐱=(x1,…,xn)\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_n), write the corresponding values as x‾\bar x and s2s^2.

The population parameter θ\theta is fixed but unknown. An estimand is the population quantity of interest, possibly a function of the parameter. An estimator is a random variable, for example θ̂=T(X1,…,Xn)\widehat{\theta}=T(X_1,\ldots,X_n); its observed numerical value T(𝐱)T(\mathbf{x}) is the estimate. Bias and variance refer to the estimator’s repeated-sampling distribution, not to one estimate.

For observed data 𝐱\mathbf{x}, define the likelihood L(θ;𝐱)L(\theta;\mathbf{x}) on a stated parameter space Θ\Theta. The log-likelihood is ℓ(θ;𝐱):=log⁡L(θ;𝐱)\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}):=\log L(\theta;\mathbf{x}), and an MLE is a maximiser over θ∈Θ\theta\in\Theta, including boundary checks when relevant.

Use H0H_0 and H1H_1 for null and alternative hypotheses. The significance level α\alpha is the Type I error probability under H0H_0; at a specified alternative, β(θ)\beta(\theta) is the Type II error probability and 1−β(θ)1-\beta(\theta) is power. A pp-value is, under H0H_0, the probability of a result at least as incompatible with H0H_0 as the observed result, using the stated statistic and tail convention. It is not the probability that H0H_0 is true. A confidence interval must name its confidence level and method; write the random interval as [L(𝐗),U(𝐗)][L(\mathbf{X}),U(\mathbf{X})] and report numerical endpoints after observing data.

Final checks

  • Domains, endpoint conventions, dimensions, parameter restrictions and distribution supports are visible wherever they affect validity.
  • Vectors and gradients are columns; a Jacobian of ℝn→ℝm\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m is m×nm\times n; chain-rule factors multiply in that order.
  • Infinite sums carry a convergence condition, and log means the natural logarithm unless a base is written.
  • Equality multipliers follow the displayed constraint encoding; KKT uses maximisation, gj≥0g_j\ge0, λj≥0\lambda_j\ge0, feasibility and complementary slackness.
  • Upper-case random variables are separated from lower-case observations; iid means independent and identically distributed; S2S^2 uses n−1n-1.
  • A pp-value is a tail probability under H0H_0, and every confidence interval states its level and method.
