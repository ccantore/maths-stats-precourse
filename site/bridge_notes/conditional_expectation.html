Conditional expectation: from joint distributions to conditional averages

This bridge note supports learning outcome S5. It closes the notation and calculation gap between a joint PMF or PDF and conditional expectation; it does not replace the assigned Week 5 reading. Throughout, XX and YY are real-valued random variables on a sample space Ω\Omega, while xx and yy denote their realised values. Write ℙ(A)\mathbb{P}(A) for the probability of an event AA, 𝔼[X]\mathbb{E}[X] for the expectation of XX, and Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(X) for its variance.

1. Start with the joint support

For discrete XX and YY, the joint PMF is

pX,Y(x,y):=ℙ(X=x,Y=y). p_{X,Y}(x,y):=\mathbb{P}(X=x,Y=y).

Its (positive-probability) joint support is

SX,Y:={(x,y):pX,Y(x,y)>0}. S_{X,Y}:=\{(x,y):p_{X,Y}(x,y)>0\}.

The marginal PMFs are obtained by summing over the other coordinate:

pX(x):=∑ypX,Y(x,y),pY(y):=∑xpX,Y(x,y). p_X(x):=\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y), \qquad p_Y(y):=\sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y).

Write SX:={x:pX(x)>0}S_X:=\{x:p_X(x)>0\} and SY:={y:pY(y)>0}S_Y:=\{y:p_Y(y)>0\} for the marginal supports.

The sums run over the joint support (terms outside it are zero). A valid PMF is non-negative and sums to one; check these facts before conditioning. For a fixed yy, the relevant conditional support is the slice

SX∣Y=y:={x:(x,y)∈SX,Y}. S_{X\mid Y=y}:=\{x:(x,y)\in S_{X,Y}\}.

For continuous XX and YY, let fX,Y(x,y)f_{X,Y}(x,y) be their joint PDF and define the operational support

SX,Y:={(x,y)∈ℝ2:fX,Y(x,y)>0}. S_{X,Y}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:f_{X,Y}(x,y)>0\}.

The marginal PDFs are the corresponding slices, written with integrals over ℝ\mathbb{R}:

fX(x):=∫ℝfX,Y(x,y)dy,fY(y):=∫ℝfX,Y(x,y)dx. f_X(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y, \qquad f_Y(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

When setting limits, use the inequalities that describe SX,YS_{X,Y}. For a fixed yy, define SX∣Y=y:={x:(x,y)∈SX,Y}S_{X\mid Y=y}:=\{x:(x,y)\in S_{X,Y}\}. The density must be non-negative and integrate to one over its joint support.

2. Conditional PMFs and PDFs

Discrete case

If pY(y)>0p_Y(y)>0, the conditional PMF of XX given Y=yY=y is

pX∣Y(x∣y):=ℙ(X=x∣Y=y)=pX,Y(x,y)pY(y),x∈SX∣Y=y. p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y):=\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid Y=y) =\frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}, \qquad x\in S_{X\mid Y=y}.

The denominator is the marginal probability of the conditioning value. A conditional PMF is not defined by this formula when pY(y)=0p_Y(y)=0; omit such a value rather than dividing by zero. For every admissible yy, verify

∑x∈SX∣Y=ypX∣Y(x∣y)=1. \sum_{x\in S_{X\mid Y=y}}p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=1.

Continuous case

If fY(y)>0f_Y(y)>0, the conditional PDF is

fX∣Y(x∣y):=fX,Y(x,y)fY(y),x∈SX∣Y=y. f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y):= \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}, \qquad x\in S_{X\mid Y=y}.

It is zero outside that conditional slice, and it integrates to one over the slice. The condition fY(y)>0f_Y(y)>0 is the density analogue of a positive conditioning probability. For values of yy with fY(y)=0f_Y(y)=0, this ratio is not used. In a continuous model, the event Y=yY=y itself has probability zero, so the conditional PDF is the appropriate operational definition.

3. Two meanings of conditional expectation

Assume XX is integrable, meaning

𝔼[|X|]<∞. \mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty.

For a fixed admissible yy, the conditional expectation is a number. In the discrete case, the global condition above makes the following sum finite for every y∈SYy\in S_Y:

m(y):=𝔼[X∣Y=y]:=∑x∈SX∣Y=yxpX∣Y(x∣y), m(y):=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] :=\sum_{x\in S_{X\mid Y=y}}x\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y),

In the continuous case, the displayed integral defines m(y)m(y) for fYf_Y-almost every yy with fY(y)>0f_Y(y)>0:

m(y):=𝔼[X∣Y=y]:=∫SX∣Y=yxfX∣Y(x∣y)dx. m(y):=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] :=\int_{S_{X\mid Y=y}}x\,f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Set m(y):=0m(y):=0 on the exceptional set if a value is needed. The particular choice there does not affect any probability or expectation because that set has zero probability under YY.

The notation m(y)m(y) emphasises that the value depends on the fixed number yy. The conditional-expectation random variable is instead

𝔼[X∣Y]:=m(Y)almost surely. \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]:=m(Y)\quad\text{almost surely}.

For a discrete YY, it takes the value m(y)m(y) when Y=yY=y. Thus 𝔼[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] is a scalar indexed by yy, whereas 𝔼[X∣Y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] is a random variable whose randomness comes from YY. Do not report one in place of the other.

Conditioning on an event

Let B⊆ΩB\subseteq\Omega be an event with ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0. For ω∈Ω\omega\in\Omega, define its indicator by

𝟙B(ω):={1,ω∈B,0,ω∉B. \mathbb{1}_B(\omega):= \begin{cases}1,&\omega\in B,\\0,&\omega\notin B.\end{cases}

For integrable XX, conditioning on BB gives the scalar

𝔼[X∣B]:=𝔼[X𝟙B]ℙ(B). \mathbb{E}[X\mid B] :=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X\mathbb{1}_B]}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.

In the discrete case B={Y=y}B=\{Y=y\} reproduces 𝔼[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] when pY(y)>0p_Y(y)>0. If B1,…,BkB_1,\ldots,B_k are disjoint events that partition Ω\Omega, each with positive probability, then the event version of total expectation is

𝔼[X]=∑i=1k𝔼[X∣Bi]ℙ(Bi). \mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[X\mid B_i]\,\mathbb{P}(B_i).

4. Independence: what follows, and what does not

The factorisation test is exact. Discrete XX and YY are independent if

pX,Y(x,y)=pX(x)pY(y)for every x,y, p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y) \quad\text{for every }x,y,

and jointly continuous XX and YY are independent if

fX,Y(x,y)=fX(x)fY(y)for two-dimensional Lebesgue-a.e. (x,y), f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y) \quad\text{for two-dimensional Lebesgue-a.e. }(x,y),

that is, outside a set of two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. Equivalently, in the discrete case pX∣Y(x∣y)=pX(x)p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=p_X(x) for every xx and every yy with pY(y)>0p_Y(y)>0; in the continuous case,

fX∣Y(x∣y)=fX(x)for one-dimensional Lebesgue-a.e. x,for fY-a.e. y with fY(y)>0. f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=f_X(x) \quad\text{for one-dimensional Lebesgue-a.e. }x, \quad\text{for }f_Y\text{-a.e. }y\text{ with }f_Y(y)>0.

Checking one cell or one conditioning value is not enough to establish independence.

If XX and YY are independent and XX is integrable, then

𝔼[X∣Y=y]=𝔼[X]and𝔼[X∣Y]=𝔼[X] \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\mathbb{E}[X] \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]

for all admissible conditioning values (or for fYf_Y-a.e. such value in the continuous case). The converse is weaker: a constant conditional mean only says that XX is mean-independent of YY, not that their full conditional distribution factorises. Likewise, zero covariance, when defined, does not by itself establish independence.

5. Iterated expectation and total variance

Law of iterated expectation

For integrable XX, the law of iterated expectation is

𝔼[𝔼[X∣Y]]=𝔼[X]. \boxed{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\right]=\mathbb{E}[X].}

For a discrete YY, this means

𝔼[X]=∑y∈SYm(y)pY(y), \mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{y\in S_Y}m(y)p_Y(y),

and for a continuous YY,

𝔼[X]=∫ℝm(y)fY(y)dy. \mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(y)f_Y(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

With 𝔼[|X|]<∞\mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty, this integral is well-defined for the version of mm above: changing mm on an fYf_Y-null set does not change it, and ∫m(y)fY(y)dy=∬xfX∣Y(x∣y)fY(y)dxdy=∬xfX,Y(x,y)dxdy=𝔼[X]. \int m(y)f_Y(y)\,\mathrm{d}y =\iint x f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y =\iint x f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y =\mathbb{E}[X].

The weights are the marginal distribution of the conditioning variable, not equal weights unless that happens to be the model.

Law of total variance

Now assume the finite-second-moment condition

𝔼[X2]<∞. \mathbb{E}[X^2]<\infty.

In the discrete case, the global second-moment condition makes the following conditional variance finite for every y∈SYy\in S_Y:

v(y):=Var⁡(X∣Y=y):=∑x∈SX∣Y=y(x−m(y))2pX∣Y(x∣y), v(y):=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y=y) :=\sum_{x\in S_{X\mid Y=y}}(x-m(y))^2p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y),

In the continuous case, define

v(y):=∫SX∣Y=y(x−m(y))2fX∣Y(x∣y)dx v(y):=\int_{S_{X\mid Y=y}}(x-m(y))^2 f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,\mathrm{d}x

for fYf_Y-almost every yy with fY(y)>0f_Y(y)>0, and set v(y):=0v(y):=0 elsewhere. Then

Var⁡(X∣Y):=v(Y)almost surely. \operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y):=v(Y)\quad\text{almost surely}.

Let μ:=𝔼[X]\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X]. Since 𝔼[X∣Y]=m(Y)\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=m(Y), the decomposition

X−μ=(X−m(Y))+(m(Y)−μ) X-\mu=\bigl(X-m(Y)\bigr)+\bigl(m(Y)-\mu\bigr)

has a conditional cross-term with expectation zero. Taking conditional and then unconditional expectations gives

Var⁡(X)=𝔼[𝔼[(X−μ)2∣Y]]=𝔼[v(Y)+(m(Y)−μ)2]=𝔼[Var⁡(X∣Y)]+Var⁡(𝔼[X∣Y]). \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mu)^2\mid Y\right]\right]\\ &=\mathbb{E}\!\left[v(Y)+\bigl(m(Y)-\mu\bigr)^2\right]\\ &=\boxed{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)] +\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\right).} \end{aligned}

The first term is average variation within the conditional groups. The second term is variation between their conditional means. Both terms are needed.

6. Worked example — one table, all the objects

Let X∈{0,1}X\in\{0,1\} and Y∈{0,1}Y\in\{0,1\} have the joint PMF below. All four cells are in the joint support.

x∖yx\backslash y 00 11
00 1/41/4 1/81/8
11 1/41/4 3/83/8

The marginals are

pY(0)=pY(1)=12,pX(0)=38,pX(1)=58. p_Y(0)=p_Y(1)=\frac12, \qquad p_X(0)=\frac38, \qquad p_X(1)=\frac58.

For Y=0Y=0, divide the first column by pY(0)=1/2p_Y(0)=1/2; for Y=1Y=1, divide the second column by pY(1)=1/2p_Y(1)=1/2. Thus

ypX∣Y(0∣y)pX∣Y(1∣y)m(y)=𝔼[X∣Y=y]01/21/21/211/43/43/4 \begin{array}{c|cc|c} y & p_{X\mid Y}(0\mid y)&p_{X\mid Y}(1\mid y)&m(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\\ \hline 0&1/2&1/2&1/2\\ 1&1/4&3/4&3/4 \end{array}

The conditional-expectation random variable is therefore

𝔼[X∣Y]={1/2,Y=0,3/4,Y=1. \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]= \begin{cases} 1/2,&Y=0,\\ 3/4,&Y=1. \end{cases}

The variables are not independent because, for example,

pX,Y(1,1)=38≠pX(1)pY(1)=58⋅12=516. p_{X,Y}(1,1)=\frac38\ne p_X(1)p_Y(1)=\frac58\cdot\frac12=\frac5{16}.

Iterated expectation gives

𝔼[𝔼[X∣Y]]=12⋅12+34⋅12=58, \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]] =\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac34\cdot\frac12 =\frac58,

which agrees with 𝔼[X]=pX(1)=5/8\mathbb{E}[X]=p_X(1)=5/8. Conditioning on the event B={Y=1}B=\{Y=1\} gives the same scalar, 𝔼[X∣B]=3/4\mathbb{E}[X\mid B]=3/4, because ℙ(B)=1/2\mathbb{P}(B)=1/2.

For total variance, the conditional Bernoulli variances are

v(0)=1212=14,v(1)=3414=316. v(0)=\frac12\frac12=\frac14, \qquad v(1)=\frac34\frac14=\frac3{16}.

Hence

𝔼[Var⁡(X∣Y)]=12⋅14+12⋅316=732, \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)] =\frac12\cdot\frac14+\frac12\cdot\frac3{16} =\frac7{32},

while

Var⁡(𝔼[X∣Y])=(12⋅14+12⋅916)−(58)2=164. \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]) =\left(\frac12\cdot\frac14+\frac12\cdot\frac9{16}\right) -\left(\frac58\right)^2 =\frac1{64}.

The total is 7/32+1/64=15/647/32+1/64=15/64. Directly, XX is marginally Bernoulli⁡(5/8)\operatorname{Bernoulli}(5/8), so

Var⁡(X)=5838=1564, \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac58\frac38=\frac{15}{64},

which verifies the decomposition.

7. Pitfalls and final check

  • Write the joint support first. Marginalise over the other variable, and carry the resulting conditional slice into every sum or integral.
  • Divide by pY(y)p_Y(y) or fY(y)f_Y(y), not by the wrong marginal and never by a zero denominator.
  • Keep 𝔼[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] (a number for fixed yy) separate from 𝔼[X∣Y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] (the random variable obtained by substituting YY).
  • In iterated expectation and total variance, weight conditional quantities by the marginal distribution of YY.
  • Total variance has a within-group term and a between-means term; dropping either changes the answer.
  • Independence implies a constant conditional distribution and conditional mean. A single matching cell, a constant conditional mean or zero covariance is not a converse proof of independence.
  • Before using an expectation, check 𝔼[|X|]<∞\mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty; before using a variance decomposition, check 𝔼[X2]<∞\mathbb{E}[X^2]<\infty.
