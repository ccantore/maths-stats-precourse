W02-Q05-PA (M4)

Method and conditions. A single counterexample is enough to disprove a universal claim. Choose

𝐏=[1000],𝐐=[0010]. \mathbf{P}= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}= \begin{bmatrix} 0&0\\1&0 \end{bmatrix}.

rank⁡(𝐏)=rank⁡(𝐐)=1. \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P})= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Q})=1.

Their product is

𝐏𝐐=[0000], \mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0\\0&0 \end{bmatrix},

which has rank zero.

In the reverse order,

𝐐𝐏=[0010], \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0\\1&0 \end{bmatrix},

which is non-zero and has rank one.

Final result and interpretation. Here

rank⁡(𝐏𝐐)=0≠1=min⁡{rank⁡(𝐏),rank⁡(𝐐)}. \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q})=0 \ne1 =\min\{\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P}), \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Q})\}.

The example therefore disproves the claim.

It also shows why order matters: 𝐏\mathbf{P} kills the second-coordinate output of 𝐐\mathbf{Q}, whereas applying 𝐏\mathbf{P} first leaves a first-coordinate input that 𝐐\mathbf{Q} sends to a non-zero vector.

(A∪B)c=Ac∩Bc, (A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c,

the event that neither is selected. Because order is irrelevant, there are

(52)=10 \binom{5}{2}=10

equally likely pairs. The complement of selecting at least one of Alice and Benoit is selecting both representatives from the other three students. There are

(32)=3 \binom{3}{2}=3

such pairs. Hence the favourable count is 10−3=710-3=7.

Thus

ℙ(A∪B)=1−(32)(52)=1−310=710. \mathbb{P}(A\cup B) =1-\frac{\binom{3}{2}}{\binom{5}{2}} =1-\frac{3}{10} =\frac{7}{10}.

For a direct count without double-counting, partition the favourable pairs into disjoint cases. Exactly one of Alice and Benoit is selected in 2(31)=62\binom{3}{1}=6 ways, and both are selected in one way. This gives 6+1=76+1=7 favourable pairs and the same probability.

Final result.

ℙ(A∪B)=710. \mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\frac{7}{10}.
