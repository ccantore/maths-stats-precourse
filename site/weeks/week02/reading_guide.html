Week 2 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • M4: perform matrix operations; compute simple determinants and inverses; use Gaussian elimination; compute rank; solve a linear system; and classify a system as having a unique solution, no solution or multiple solutions; and
  • S2: compute and interpret conditional probabilities; use or test independence; and apply the law of total probability and Bayes’ rule.

Prerequisites and retrieval

You will use algebra and functions from M2 and event/probability rules from S1 throughout the new material. The final question contains three explicit Week 1 retrieval parts on logarithms (M2), Taylor approximation (M3) and set notation with elementary counting/probability (M1, S1).

Before starting, check that you can rearrange a linear equation, multiply signed numbers and fractions, use set notation for events, and apply the addition rule for two events.

Student materials

  1. Read the exact external sections and skip instructions in the reading guide.

  4. Use the progressive hints only after a serious attempt.
  5. Mark your work with the complete solutions, record errors by outcome ID, and reattempt missed parts after two or three days.

The required external readings are Jim Hefferon’s Linear Algebra, at the listed chapter and subsection locators, and MIT OpenCourseWare 18.05 Class 3 Reading, §§2–7.2. Do not read or attempt the excluded software, video or out-of-scope sections named in reading_guide.md.

Suggested study sequence

  1. Review matrix dimensions and multiplication.
  2. Work through Gaussian elimination, pivots and rank.
  3. Connect rank to the three possible classifications of a linear system.
  4. Review conditional probability, then derive total probability and Bayes’ rule from the multiplication rule.
  5. Alternate linear-algebra and probability questions rather than completing one entire strand first.
  6. Finish with the Week 1 retrieval block, mark all work, and update your error log.

The notes and examples below are original course material. All vectors are column vectors.

Matrices and their operations

An m×nm\times n matrix has mm rows and nn columns. Two matrices can be added only when their dimensions are the same. If 𝐀\mathbf{A} is m×nm\times n and 𝐁\mathbf{B} is n×pn\times p, then 𝐀𝐁\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} exists and is m×pm\times p. Its entry in row ii and column jj is

(𝐀𝐁)ij=∑k=1naikbkj. (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{ij} =\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}b_{kj}.

The order matters. Even when both products exist, 𝐀𝐁\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} and 𝐁𝐀\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} need not be equal and may have different dimensions.

The transpose 𝐀𝖳\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T} interchanges rows and columns. Thus, if 𝐀\mathbf{A} is m×nm\times n, then 𝐀𝖳\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T} is n×mn\times m.

Example: checking dimensions before multiplying

Let

𝐀=[1−12031],𝐛=[21−1]. \mathbf{A}= \begin{bmatrix} 1&-1&2\\ 0&3&1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b}= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\1\\-1 \end{bmatrix}.

Method. The matrix is 2×32\times3 and the vector is 3×13\times1, so 𝐀𝐛\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b} exists and is 2×12\times1.

Solution. Row-by-column multiplication gives

𝐀𝐛=[1(2)+(−1)(1)+2(−1)0(2)+3(1)+1(−1)]=[−12]. \mathbf{A}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(2)+(-1)(1)+2(-1)\\ 0(2)+3(1)+1(-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1\\2 \end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation. A dimension check prevents an undefined product and also predicts the dimension of the answer.

Linear systems and Gaussian elimination

A system of mm linear equations in nn unknowns can be written

𝐀𝐱=𝐛, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},

where 𝐀\mathbf{A} is m×nm\times n, 𝐱∈ℝn\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n, and 𝐛∈ℝm\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m. The augmented matrix is [𝐀∣𝐛][\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{b}].

The elementary row operations are:

  1. interchange two rows;
  2. multiply a row by a non-zero scalar; and
  3. add a scalar multiple of one row to another row.

These operations preserve the solution set. Gaussian elimination uses them to reach row-echelon form. A leading non-zero entry in a non-zero row is a pivot. Back-substitution then recovers the unknowns. Gauss–Jordan elimination continues until each pivot is the only non-zero entry in its column.

Example: a system with a free variable

Consider

x+2y−z=3,2x+y+z=4,3x+3y=7. \begin{aligned} x+2y-z&=3,\\ 2x+y+z&=4,\\ 3x+3y&=7. \end{aligned}

Method. Form the augmented matrix and eliminate xx from rows 2 and 3.

Solution. Begin with

[12−1321143307]. \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&2&-1&3\\ 2&1&1&4\\ 3&3&0&7 \end{array} \right].

Apply R2←R2−2R1R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1 and R3←R3−3R1R_3\leftarrow R_3-3R_1:

[12−130−33−20−33−2]. \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&2&-1&3\\ 0&-3&3&-2\\ 0&-3&3&-2 \end{array} \right].

Then R3←R3−R2R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_2, so the third row is zero. There are only two pivots, not three. Let z=t∈ℝz=t\in\mathbb{R}. The second row gives −3y+3t=−2-3y+3t=-2, so y=t+2/3y=t+2/3. The first row gives x=5/3−tx=5/3-t. Hence

𝐱=[5/3−t2/3+tt],t∈ℝ. \mathbf{x}= \begin{bmatrix} 5/3-t\\ 2/3+t\\ t \end{bmatrix}, \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Interpretation. An apparently square system can still have multiple solutions. The number of equations alone does not determine solvability.

Rank and solvability

The rank of a matrix is the number of pivots in any row-echelon form. Write it as rank⁡(𝐀)\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}). For a system with nn unknowns:

  • if rank⁡(𝐀)≠rank⁡([𝐀∣𝐛])\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\ne \operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{b}]), the system has no solution;
  • if the two ranks are equal to nn, the system has a unique solution; and
  • if the two ranks are equal but are less than nn, the system has multiple solutions, with at least one free variable.

A row of the form

[0⋯0∣c],c≠0, \begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0\mid c\end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\ne0,

is a contradiction. It increases the augmented rank but not the coefficient rank, so the system is inconsistent.

Example: a parameter changes consistency

Suppose elimination gives

[102101−13000θ−4]. \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&0&2&1\\ 0&1&-1&3\\ 0&0&0&\theta-4 \end{array} \right].

If θ≠4\theta\ne4, the last row is a contradiction, so the system has no solution. If θ=4\theta=4, the last row is zero. There are two pivots and three unknowns, so the consistent system has multiple solutions.

Determinants and inverses

For a 2×22\times2 matrix,

𝐀=[abcd],det⁡(𝐀)=ad−bc. \mathbf{A}= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\c&d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{A})=ad-bc.

The matrix is invertible exactly when det⁡(𝐀)≠0\det(\mathbf{A})\ne0. In that case,

𝐀−1=1ad−bc[d−b−ca]. \mathbf{A}^{-1} =\frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d&-b\\-c&a \end{bmatrix}.

When 𝐀\mathbf{A} is an invertible n×nn\times n matrix, the system 𝐀𝐱=𝐛\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} has the unique solution 𝐱=𝐀−1𝐛\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}. Elimination remains the more general method because it also diagnoses singular and non-square systems.

Conditional probability

For events AA and BB with ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0, the conditional probability of AA given BB is

ℙ(A∣B)=ℙ(A∩B)ℙ(B). \mathbb{P}(A\mid B) =\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.

Rearranging gives the multiplication rule,

ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(A∣B)ℙ(B). \mathbb{P}(A\cap B) =\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\mathbb{P}(B).

Conditioning changes the relevant reference group. The denominator is the probability of the event after the vertical bar.

Independence

Events AA and BB are independent when

ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(A)ℙ(B). \mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B).

If both conditioning events have positive probability, equivalent statements are ℙ(A∣B)=ℙ(A)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A) and ℙ(B∣A)=ℙ(B)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=\mathbb{P}(B). Independence is not the same as disjointness. Two disjoint events with positive probability cannot be independent because their intersection has probability zero while the product of their probabilities is positive.

Total probability and Bayes’ rule

Let B1,…,BkB_1,\ldots,B_k be a partition of Ω\Omega: the events are pairwise disjoint, their union is Ω\Omega, and each has positive probability. The law of total probability states that

ℙ(A)=∑i=1kℙ(A∣Bi)ℙ(Bi). \mathbb{P}(A) =\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\mathbb{P}(B_i).

For any jj with ℙ(A)>0\mathbb{P}(A)>0, Bayes’ rule gives

ℙ(Bj∣A)=ℙ(A∣Bj)ℙ(Bj)∑i=1kℙ(A∣Bi)ℙ(Bi). \mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A) =\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_j)\mathbb{P}(B_j)} {\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\mathbb{P}(B_i)}.

The numerator is the joint probability of AA and BjB_j. The denominator adds the joint probabilities for every route by which AA can occur.

Example: total probability and reversal of conditioning

A department offers a morning session to 3/53/5 of students and an evening session to 2/52/5. Let MM and EE denote the two session events, and let CC denote completing a diagnostic correctly. Suppose

ℙ(C∣M)=23,ℙ(C∣E)=12. \mathbb{P}(C\mid M)=\frac{2}{3}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(C\mid E)=\frac{1}{2}.

Method. The events MM and EE form a partition. First find ℙ(C)\mathbb{P}(C), then reverse the conditioning with Bayes’ rule.

Solution. Total probability gives

ℙ(C)=2335+1225=35. \mathbb{P}(C) =\frac{2}{3}\frac{3}{5}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{2}{5} =\frac{3}{5}.

Therefore,

ℙ(M∣C)=ℙ(C∣M)ℙ(M)ℙ(C)=(2/3)(3/5)3/5=23. \mathbb{P}(M\mid C) =\frac{\mathbb{P}(C\mid M)\mathbb{P}(M)}{\mathbb{P}(C)} =\frac{(2/3)(3/5)}{3/5} =\frac{2}{3}.

Interpretation. The conditional probability must be computed in the direction asked. In general, ℙ(M∣C)\mathbb{P}(M\mid C) is not the same as ℙ(C∣M)\mathbb{P}(C\mid M), even though they happen to be numerically equal in this example.

Common pitfalls

  • Check matrix dimensions before multiplying.
  • Record row operations so an arithmetic error can be located.
  • Do not infer uniqueness merely because the system is square.
  • Compare the coefficient and augmented ranks before classifying a system.
  • Read ℙ(A∣B)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B) as “probability of AA, given BB”.
  • Do not interchange ℙ(A∣B)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B) and ℙ(B∣A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A).
  • Test independence with a valid equality; do not infer it from a verbal impression.
  • In total probability, verify that the conditioning events form a partition.
