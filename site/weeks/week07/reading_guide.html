Week 7 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • solve equality-constrained optimisation problems with Lagrange multipliers, interpret a multiplier under the stated constraint encoding, and apply the envelope theorem (M10);
  • formulate and solve elementary inequality-constrained optimisation problems using the course Kuhn–Tucker convention, complementary slackness and explicit interior and corner checks (M11); and
  • distinguish parameters, estimands, estimators and estimates; evaluate elementary estimators by bias, variance, mean squared error and consistency; and derive simple method-of-moments and maximum-likelihood estimators (S7).

Prerequisites and retrieval

You will use curvature and sufficiency conditions from M8, unconstrained optimisation from M9, moments from S4, and sampling results from S6. Before beginning, retrieve the column-gradient convention, the negative-definite Hessian test, the distinction between a candidate and a global solution, and Var⁡(X‾)=Var⁡(X1)/n\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)/n for an iid sample with finite variance.

Question 10 contains three explicit cumulative retrieval parts on M8, M9 and S6. Complete that block without consulting earlier notes.

Course convention for inequality constraints

For every maximisation problem, write inequality constraints as gj(𝐱)≥0g_j(\mathbf{x})\ge0 and use

ℒ(𝐱,𝛌,𝛍)=f(𝐱)+∑jλjgj(𝐱)+∑kμkhk(𝐱),λj≥0. \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}) =f(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_j\lambda_jg_j(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_k\mu_kh_k(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \lambda_j\ge0.

The required conditions are stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility and complementary slackness. Write non-negativity restrictions such as xi≥0x_i\ge0 as explicit constraints.

Equality constraints and multipliers (M10)

Consider the maximisation problem

max⁡𝐱∈ℝnf(𝐱,𝐚)subject tohk(𝐱,𝐚)=0,k=1,…,m. \max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n} f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}) \quad\text{subject to}\quad h_k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})=0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,m.

Under the course convention, the Lagrangian is

ℒ(𝐱,𝛍;𝐚)=f(𝐱,𝐚)+∑k=1mμkhk(𝐱,𝐚). \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{a}) =f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}) +\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_kh_k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}).

At a regular local optimum, the equality-constraint gradients must have the required linear independence, and the first-order conditions are

∇𝐱ℒ(𝐱,𝛍;𝐚)=𝟎,hk(𝐱,𝐚)=0. \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} (\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{a}) =\mathbf{0}, \qquad h_k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})=0.

These conditions identify candidates. A separate argument is needed for a maximum. For example, if ff is strictly concave and the equality constraints are affine, a feasible first-order candidate is the unique global maximiser.

A multiplier’s sign has no interpretation until the constraint encoding is stated. If a scalar resource parameter aa enters as h(𝐱,a)=a−r(𝐱)=0h(\mathbf{x},a)=a-r(\mathbf{x})=0, increasing aa changes the required equality level. It relaxes a resource constraint only when this equality is the binding form of an associated restriction r(𝐱)≤ar(\mathbf{x})\le a. Under suitable regularity and differentiability conditions, if aa enters the problem only through this constraint (so fa=0f_a=0), the envelope theorem gives

dVda=∂ℒ∂a|(𝐱*(a),𝛍*(a))=μ*(a). \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}a} = \left. \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a} \right|_{(\mathbf{x}^*(a),\boldsymbol{\mu}^*(a))} =\mu^*(a).

If the same feasible set were encoded as r(𝐱)−a=0r(\mathbf{x})-a=0, the multiplier would have the opposite sign.

Example 1 — a nonlinear equality constraint and envelope derivative (M10)

For a>0a>0, solve

max⁡x,y{x+y}subject tox2+2y2=a. \max_{x,y}\{x+y\} \quad\text{subject to}\quad x^2+2y^2=a.

Find the value function and verify the envelope derivative.

Method. Encode the constraint as h(x,y,a)=a−x2−2y2=0h(x,y,a)=a-x^2-2y^2=0, solve the regular first-order system, compare all candidates on the compact feasible ellipse, and then apply the envelope theorem.

Solution. The Lagrangian is

ℒ(x,y,μ;a)=x+y+μ(a−x2−2y2). \mathcal{L}(x,y,\mu;a) =x+y+\mu(a-x^2-2y^2).

The first-order and feasibility conditions are

1−2μx=0,1−4μy=0,a−x2−2y2=0. 1-2\mu x=0, \qquad 1-4\mu y=0, \qquad a-x^2-2y^2=0.

The first two conditions imply x=2yx=2y. Feasibility then gives the two candidates

(x,y)=(2a6,a6)and(x,y)=(−2a6,−a6). (x,y) = \left(2\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}},\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}}\right) \quad\text{and}\quad (x,y) = \left(-2\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}},-\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}}\right).

The constraint gradient is non-zero everywhere on the ellipse because a>0a>0. The feasible set is compact, so a global maximum exists, and the two candidate objective values are respectively 3a/63\sqrt{a/6} and −3a/6-3\sqrt{a/6}. Therefore

x*(a)=2a6,y*(a)=a6,μ*(a)=64a. x^*(a)=2\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}}, \qquad y^*(a)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}}, \qquad \mu^*(a)=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{a}}.

The value function is

V(a)=3a6=3a2, V(a)=3\sqrt{\frac{a}{6}}=\sqrt{\frac{3a}{2}},

so direct differentiation gives

V′(a)=64a. V'(a)=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{a}}.

The envelope calculation agrees:

∂ℒ∂a|*=μ*(a)=64a. \left. \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a} \right|_* =\mu^*(a) =\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{a}}.

Interpretation. Because the constraint was written a−x2−2y2=0a-x^2-2y^2=0, the positive multiplier measures the marginal value of increasing the quadratic-capacity level aa. It is a relaxation only when the equality is the binding form of the associated capacity restriction x2+2y2≤ax^2+2y^2\le a. Reversing the constraint encoding reverses the multiplier sign but leaves V′(a)V'(a) unchanged.

Inequality constraints and the course convention (M11)

The course-wide convention is a maximisation problem with

gj(𝐱)≥0,hk(𝐱)=0. g_j(\mathbf{x})\ge0, \qquad h_k(\mathbf{x})=0.

Use

ℒ(𝐱,𝛌,𝛍)=f(𝐱)+∑jλjgj(𝐱)+∑kμkhk(𝐱),λj≥0. \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}) =f(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_j\lambda_jg_j(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_k\mu_kh_k(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \lambda_j\ge0.

The Kuhn–Tucker conditions are:

  1. Stationarity

    ∇f(𝐱)+∑jλj∇gj(𝐱)+∑kμk∇hk(𝐱)=𝟎. \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_j\lambda_j\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}) +\sum_k\mu_k\nabla h_k(\mathbf{x}) =\mathbf{0}.

  2. Primal feasibility

    gj(𝐱)≥0,hk(𝐱)=0. g_j(\mathbf{x})\ge0, \qquad h_k(\mathbf{x})=0.

  3. Dual feasibility

    λj≥0. \lambda_j\ge0.

  4. Complementary slackness

    λjgj(𝐱)=0. \lambda_jg_j(\mathbf{x})=0.

Complementary slackness says that a slack constraint has a zero multiplier. It does not say that every zero multiplier corresponds to a slack constraint. For the elementary problems in this week, strict concavity of the objective and affine constraints provide a direct sufficiency route once a feasible Kuhn–Tucker point has been found.

Example 2 — a two-variable lower-bound corner (M11)

Solve

max⁡x,y−(x+2)2−2(y−1)2subject tox≥0,y≥0. \max_{x,y}\; -(x+2)^2-2(y-1)^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0.

Method. Treat both non-negativity restrictions as explicit gj(x,y)≥0g_j(x,y)\ge0 constraints. Use complementary slackness to test the active lower-bound branch.

Solution. Define

g1(x,y)=x,g2(x,y)=y. g_1(x,y)=x, \qquad g_2(x,y)=y.

The Lagrangian is

ℒ(x,y,λ1,λ2)=−(x+2)2−2(y−1)2+λ1x+λ2y. \mathcal{L}(x,y,\lambda_1,\lambda_2) =-(x+2)^2-2(y-1)^2+\lambda_1x+\lambda_2y.

The complete conditions are

−2(x+2)+λ1=0,−4(y−1)+λ2=0, -2(x+2)+\lambda_1=0, \qquad -4(y-1)+\lambda_2=0,

x≥0,y≥0,λ1≥0,λ2≥0, x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0,\qquad \lambda_1\ge0,\qquad\lambda_2\ge0,

λ1x=0,λ2y=0. \lambda_1x=0, \qquad \lambda_2y=0.

The unconstrained maximiser (−2,1)(-2,1) violates x≥0x\ge0. On the branch x=0x=0 and y>0y>0, complementary slackness gives λ2=0\lambda_2=0. Stationarity then gives

y=1,λ1=4. y=1, \qquad \lambda_1=4.

All primal, dual and complementary-slackness conditions hold, so

(x*,y*)=(0,1),(λ1*,λ2*)=(4,0). (x^*,y^*)=(0,1), \qquad (\lambda_1^*,\lambda_2^*)=(4,0).

The Hessian is diag⁡(−2,−4)\operatorname{diag}(-2,-4), which is negative definite, and the feasible set is convex. The feasible Kuhn–Tucker point is therefore the unique global maximiser.

Interpretation. The lower bound on xx prevents movement towards the unconstrained maximiser. Its positive multiplier is attached to the explicit course constraint x≥0x\ge0; the slack lower bound on yy has multiplier zero.

Estimator terminology and properties (S7)

Let X1,…,Xn∼iidFθX_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}F_\theta.

  • A parameter θ\theta indexes the population model and is fixed but unknown in repeated sampling.
  • An estimand is the population quantity to be learned. It may be θ\theta itself or a function such as θ2\theta^2.
  • An estimator θ̂=T(X1,…,Xn)\widehat\theta=T(X_1,\ldots,X_n) is a random variable before the sample is observed.
  • An estimate is the realised numerical value T(x1,…,xn)T(x_1,\ldots,x_n).

The bias, variance and mean squared error are

Bias⁡θ(θ̂)=𝔼θ[θ̂]−θ, \operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\widehat\theta) =\mathbb{E}_\theta[\widehat\theta]-\theta,

Var⁡θ(θ̂), \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\widehat\theta),

and

MSE⁡θ(θ̂)=𝔼θ[(θ̂−θ)2]=Var⁡θ(θ̂)+Bias⁡θ(θ̂)2. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\widehat\theta) &=\mathbb{E}_\theta[(\widehat\theta-\theta)^2]\\ &=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\widehat\theta) +\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\widehat\theta)^2. \end{aligned}

An estimator sequence θ̂n\widehat\theta_n is consistent for θ\theta if, for every ε>0\varepsilon>0,

ℙθ(|θ̂n−θ|>ε)→0as n→∞. \mathbb{P}_\theta \left( |\widehat\theta_n-\theta|>\varepsilon \right) \longrightarrow0 \quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Unbiasedness is a finite-sample expectation statement; consistency is a large-sample probability statement. Neither property is determined by one observed estimate.

Example 3 — two unbiased estimators with different consistency (S7)

Let

X1,…,Xn∼iidBernoulli⁡(p),0<p<1. X_1,\ldots,X_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad 0<p<1.

Compare

p̂1,n=X1andp̂2,n=X‾. \widehat p_{1,n}=X_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \widehat p_{2,n}=\bar X.

Method. Compute expectation, variance and mean squared error, then apply the definition of consistency.

Solution. Both estimators are unbiased:

𝔼p[p̂1,n]=𝔼p[X1]=p \mathbb{E}_p[\widehat p_{1,n}] =\mathbb{E}_p[X_1] =p

and

𝔼p[p̂2,n]=𝔼p[X‾]=p. \mathbb{E}_p[\widehat p_{2,n}] =\mathbb{E}_p[\bar X] =p.

Their variances, and therefore their mean squared errors, are

MSE⁡p(p̂1,n)=Var⁡p(X1)=p(1−p) \operatorname{MSE}_p(\widehat p_{1,n}) =\operatorname{Var}_p(X_1) =p(1-p)

and

MSE⁡p(p̂2,n)=Var⁡p(X‾)=p(1−p)n. \operatorname{MSE}_p(\widehat p_{2,n}) =\operatorname{Var}_p(\bar X) =\frac{p(1-p)}{n}.

The second mean squared error tends to zero. For every ε>0\varepsilon>0, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

ℙp(|X‾−p|>ε)≤p(1−p)nε2→0. \mathbb{P}_p(|\bar X-p|>\varepsilon) \le \frac{p(1-p)}{n\varepsilon^2} \longrightarrow0.

Thus p̂2,n\widehat p_{2,n} is consistent.

By contrast, choose 0<ε<min⁡{p,1−p}0<\varepsilon<\min\{p,1-p\}. Since X1X_1 is either zero or one,

|X1−p|>ε |X_1-p|>\varepsilon

with probability one. Therefore p̂1,n=X1\widehat p_{1,n}=X_1 is not consistent, even though it is unbiased.

Interpretation. Increasing nn improves the sample mean because it uses the additional observations. The estimator X1X_1 ignores them, so its sampling uncertainty does not shrink.

Method of moments and maximum likelihood (S7)

The method of moments matches population moments to their sample counterparts. For a one-parameter model, use the lowest raw moment that identifies the parameter. When the finite mean m(θ)=𝔼θ[X]m(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\theta[X] identifies θ\theta, solve

m(θ̂MOM)=X‾. m(\widehat\theta_{\mathrm{MOM}})=\bar X.

If the mean does not identify θ\theta, a higher raw moment may do so. For example, a second-moment equation matches 𝔼θ[X2]\mathbb{E}_\theta[X^2] to n−1∑iXi2n^{-1}\sum_iX_i^2.

Maximum likelihood treats the observed data 𝐱=(x1,…,xn)\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_n) as fixed and maximises

L(θ;𝐱) L(\theta;\mathbf{x})

over the stated parameter space. A score equation identifies an interior candidate, not automatically the global maximum. Check curvature, monotonicity or boundary behaviour as appropriate.

Example 4 — moments and likelihood for a normal variance (S7)

Let

X1,…,Xn∼iid𝒩(0,θ),θ>0. X_1,\ldots,X_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\theta), \qquad \theta>0.

Derive the method-of-moments and maximum-likelihood estimators of the variance θ\theta. Treat separately the case in which ∑ixi2=0\sum_i x_i^2=0.

Method. The first raw moment is zero for every θ\theta, so use 𝔼θ[X2]=θ\mathbb{E}_\theta[X^2]=\theta for the moment equation. For maximum likelihood, write S2=∑ixi2S_2=\sum_i x_i^2, form the joint density, and check the score sign and both parameter-space boundaries.

Solution. Matching the second raw moment gives

θ̂MOM=1n∑i=1nXi2. \widehat\theta_{\mathrm{MOM}} =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i^2.

For any observed real values, the likelihood is

L(θ;𝐱)=(2πθ)−n/2exp⁡(−S22θ),θ>0. L(\theta;\mathbf{x}) =(2\pi\theta)^{-n/2} \exp\left( -\frac{S_2}{2\theta} \right), \qquad \theta>0.

After removing terms that do not depend on θ\theta, the log-likelihood is

ℓ(θ;𝐱)=−n2log⁡θ−S22θ+constant. \ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}) =-\frac{n}{2}\log\theta-\frac{S_2}{2\theta}+\text{constant}.

When S2>0S_2>0, the score is

∂ℓ∂θ=−n2θ+S22θ2=S2−nθ2θ2. \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta} =-\frac{n}{2\theta}+\frac{S_2}{2\theta^2} =\frac{S_2-n\theta}{2\theta^2}.

It is positive below S2/nS_2/n, zero at S2/nS_2/n, and negative above S2/nS_2/n. Moreover, the log-likelihood tends to −∞-\infty as θ↓0\theta\downarrow0 and as θ→∞\theta\to\infty. Therefore the unique global maximum is

θ̂MLE=1n∑i=1nXi2. \widehat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}} =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i^2.

If S2=0S_2=0, however, the likelihood is proportional to θ−n/2\theta^{-n/2} and is unbounded as θ↓0\theta\downarrow0. No finite MLE exists on θ>0\theta>0. The second-moment equation gives zero, which is outside the parameter space, so there is also no admissible finite method-of-moments estimate in this case.

Interpretation. On positive-squared-sum samples, the two methods happen to give the sample second raw moment in this normal variance model. This is not the sample mean used in a Poisson mean problem, and agreement between the two methods remains model-specific.
