Week 5 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • use total and implicit differentiation under stated regularity conditions, identify homogeneous functions and apply Euler’s theorem (M7);
  • classify quadratic forms by definiteness, determine whether sets are convex, and distinguish convexity, concavity and quasiconcavity using valid definitions or derivative tests (M8); and
  • derive marginal and conditional distributions from a joint distribution, assess independence, and calculate conditional expectations using iterated expectation or total variance where appropriate (S5).

Prerequisites and retrieval

Retrieve basis and eigenvalue calculations (M5), column gradients and Hessians (M6), conditional probability (S2), and expectation and variance under linear transformations (S4). Questions 9 and 10 contain three explicit closed-note retrieval parts spanning M5, S2 and S4; the current-week calculus tasks also require fresh use of M6.

Before starting, check that you can multiply a matrix by a column vector, compute a 2×22\times2 determinant, differentiate a two-variable function, normalise a simple PMF or PDF, and calculate a weighted expectation.

Suggested study sequence

  1. Write the scalar and system implicit-function conditions at the top of your notes.
  2. Study total differentiation and homogeneity, checking the orientation of every gradient and the dimensions of every Jacobian.
  3. Work through quadratic forms before moving to convex sets and curvature. Keep concavity and quasiconcavity as distinct classifications.
  4. Begin the statistics block by drawing the support of each joint distribution in words or inequalities. Derive marginals before conditionals.
  5. Reproduce the conditional-expectation and total-variance calculations, then attempt the exercise sheet in alternating mathematics and statistics blocks.
  6. Complete Questions 9 and 10 without earlier notes. Mark all work, update the error log, and use the self-test as the final closed-note check.

Example 1 — total and scalar implicit differentiation (M7; 15 minutes)

Let

C(a,b)=a2b+log⁡b,a∈ℝ,b>0. C(a,b)=a^2b+\log b, \qquad a\in\mathbb{R},\quad b>0.

Find the total differential at (a,b)=(1,2)(a,b)=(1,2) and use it to approximate the change when da=0.1\mathrm{d}a=0.1 and db=−0.05\mathrm{d}b=-0.05. Then suppose F(x,θ)=x2+θx−3=0F(x,\theta)=x^2+\theta x-3=0. Find dx/dθ\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}\theta at (x,θ)=(1,2)(x,\theta)=(1,2).

Method. Form the column gradient before taking its transpose in the total differential. For the implicit derivative, check differentiability and the non-zero partial derivative FxF_x.

Solution. The column gradient is

∇C(a,b)=[2aba2+1/b]. \nabla C(a,b)= \begin{bmatrix} 2ab\\ a^2+1/b \end{bmatrix}.

At (1,2)(1,2),

∇C(1,2)=[43/2]. \nabla C(1,2)= \begin{bmatrix} 4\\3/2 \end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

dC=∇C(1,2)𝖳[dadb]=4da+32db. \mathrm{d}C =\nabla C(1,2)^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}a\\\mathrm{d}b\end{bmatrix} =4\,\mathrm{d}a+\frac32\,\mathrm{d}b.

For the stated changes,

ΔC≈dC=4(0.1)+32(−0.05)=0.325. \Delta C\approx\mathrm{d}C =4(0.1)+\frac32(-0.05) =0.325.

The polynomial FF is continuously differentiable. At (1,2)(1,2),

Fx=2x+θ=4≠0,Fθ=x=1. F_x=2x+\theta=4\ne0, \qquad F_\theta=x=1.

The scalar implicit-function formula is valid locally, and

dxdθ|(1,2)=−FθFx=−14. \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\right|_{(1,2)} =-\frac{F_\theta}{F_x} =-\frac14.

Interpretation. A total differential is a first-order approximation, not an exact finite change. The implicit derivative is a local conclusion whose validity depends on the non-zero denominator.

Example 2 — a system derivative and Euler’s theorem (M7; 20 minutes)

The equations

F1(u,v,θ)=u+2v−θ−3=0,F2(u,v,θ)=u2+v−2=0 \begin{aligned} F_1(u,v,\theta)&=u+2v-\theta-3=0,\\ F_2(u,v,\theta)&=u^2+v-2=0 \end{aligned}

hold at (u,v,θ)=(1,1,0)(u,v,\theta)=(1,1,0). Find d[u,v]𝖳/dθ\mathrm{d}[u,v]^{\mathsf T}/\mathrm{d}\theta there. Then verify Euler’s theorem for h(x,y)=x2/yh(x,y)=x^2/y on x>0,y>0x>0,y>0.

Method. For the system, identify the 2×22\times2 Jacobian with respect to the two endogenous variables and check that it is nonsingular. For homogeneity, scale both arguments by the same positive number before applying Euler’s theorem.

Solution. The relevant derivatives are

𝐉𝐅,(u,v)=[122u1],𝐅θ=[−10]. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{F},(u,v)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\ 2u&1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{F}_\theta= \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix}.

At the stated point,

det⁡[1221]=−3≠0. \det \begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\2&1 \end{bmatrix} =-3\ne0.

Hence the implicit function exists locally and

[1221][u′(θ)v′(θ)]=−[−10]=[10]. \begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\2&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u'(\theta)\\v'(\theta) \end{bmatrix} =- \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.

Solving gives

u′(0)=−13,v′(0)=23. u'(0)=-\frac13, \qquad v'(0)=\frac23.

For t>0t>0,

h(tx,ty)=(tx)2ty=th(x,y), h(tx,ty)=\frac{(tx)^2}{ty} =t\,h(x,y),

so hh is homogeneous of degree one. It is differentiable on the stated domain, with

hx=2xy,hy=−x2y2. h_x=\frac{2x}{y}, \qquad h_y=-\frac{x^2}{y^2}.

Euler’s theorem gives

xhx+yhy=2x2y−x2y=x2y=h(x,y). xh_x+yh_y =\frac{2x^2}{y}-\frac{x^2}{y} =\frac{x^2}{y} =h(x,y).

Interpretation. Nonsingularity is not a cosmetic matrix check: it is what allows the endogenous-variable derivative to be solved uniquely. Euler’s identity expresses the degree of a differentiable homogeneous function.

Example 3 — the symmetric part of a quadratic form (M8; 15 minutes)

Classify

q(𝐱)=𝐱𝖳𝐀𝐱,𝐀=[14−23],𝐱=[xy]. q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{A}= \begin{bmatrix} 1&4\\ -2&3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x}= \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}.

Method. A quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part (𝐀+𝐀𝖳)/2(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T})/2. Apply a definiteness test to that matrix.

Solution. The symmetric part is

𝐒=𝐀+𝐀𝖳2=[1113]. \mathbf{S} =\frac{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}}{2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix}.

Its leading principal minors are

1>0,det⁡(𝐒)=3−1=2>0. 1>0, \qquad \det(\mathbf{S})=3-1=2>0.

Thus 𝐒\mathbf{S} is positive definite. Equivalently,

q(𝐱)=x2+2xy+3y2=(x+y)2+2y2>0 q(\mathbf{x}) =x^2+2xy+3y^2 =(x+y)^2+2y^2>0

for every non-zero 𝐱\mathbf{x}. The quadratic form is positive definite.

Interpretation. Applying a symmetry-based test directly to the original non-symmetric matrix would be invalid. The skew-symmetric part contributes zero to 𝐱𝖳𝐀𝐱\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}.

Example 4 — convexity and quasiconcavity are different (M8; 15 minutes)

Show that

E={(x,y)∈ℝ2:x≥0,y≥0,x+2y≤4} E=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x+2y\le4\}

is convex. On the domain ℝ++2:={(x,y):x>0,y>0}\mathbb{R}_{++}^2:=\{(x,y):x>0,y>0\}, classify g(x,y)=x/yg(x,y)=x/y as convex, concave or quasiconcave.

Method. Use the convex-combination definition for the set. Use the Hessian to assess convexity and concavity of the function, but use upper contour sets for quasiconcavity.

Solution. Take (x1,y1),(x2,y2)∈E(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)\in E and λ∈[0,1]\lambda\in[0,1]. Both coordinates of their convex combination are non-negative. Moreover,

λx1+(1−λ)x2+2(λy1+(1−λ)y2)=λ(x1+2y1)+(1−λ)(x2+2y2)≤4λ+4(1−λ)=4. \begin{aligned} &\lambda x_1+(1-\lambda)x_2 +2\bigl(\lambda y_1+(1-\lambda)y_2\bigr)\\ &\quad=\lambda(x_1+2y_1)+(1-\lambda)(x_2+2y_2) \le 4\lambda+4(1-\lambda)=4. \end{aligned}

Thus the convex combination also lies in EE, so EE is convex.

For gg,

𝐇g(x,y)=[0−1/y2−1/y22x/y3], \mathbf{H}_g(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1/y^2\\ -1/y^2&2x/y^3 \end{bmatrix},

whose determinant is

−1y4<0. -\frac{1}{y^4}<0.

The Hessian is indefinite throughout the domain, so gg is neither convex nor concave.

For c>0c>0, the upper contour set is

{(x,y)∈ℝ++2:g(x,y)≥c}={(x,y)∈ℝ++2:x−cy≥0}. \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^2:g(x,y)\ge c\} =\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^2:x-cy\ge0\}.

This is the intersection of the convex domain with a half-space, so it is convex. For c≤0c\le0, the upper contour set is the whole domain, which is convex. Therefore gg is quasiconcave.

Interpretation. Quasiconcavity concerns convex upper contour sets. It does not require a negative semidefinite Hessian and does not imply concavity.

The joint PMF of X∈{0,1,2}X\in\{0,1,2\} and Y∈{0,1}Y\in\{0,1\} is

xx pX,Y(x,0)p_{X,Y}(x,0) pX,Y(x,1)p_{X,Y}(x,1)

Find 𝔼[X∣Y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] and verify the law of iterated expectation.

Method. Sum the joint PMF to obtain the marginal of YY, divide each column by its positive marginal, and calculate the conditional means.

Solution. The marginal probabilities are

ℙ(Y=0)=12,ℙ(Y=1)=12. \mathbb{P}(Y=0)=\frac12, \qquad \mathbb{P}(Y=1)=\frac12.

Conditional on Y=0Y=0, the probabilities of X=0X=0 and X=1X=1 are each 1/21/2, so

𝔼[X∣Y=0]=12. \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=0]=\frac12.

Conditional on Y=1Y=1, the probabilities of X=1X=1 and X=2X=2 are each 1/21/2, so

𝔼[X∣Y=1]=32. \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1]=\frac32.

Thus

𝔼[X∣Y]={1/2,Y=0,3/2,Y=1. \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] = \begin{cases} 1/2,&Y=0,\\ 3/2,&Y=1. \end{cases}

𝔼[𝔼[X∣Y]]=1212+3212=1. \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]] =\frac12\frac12+\frac32\frac12 =1.

The marginal PMF of XX is

Example 6 — continuous conditioning and total variance (S5; 15 minutes)

Let the joint density be

fX,Y(x,y)=1for 0<x<1,x<y<x+1, f_{X,Y}(x,y)=1 \quad\text{for }0<x<1,\quad x<y<x+1,

and zero otherwise. Derive fY∣X(y∣x)f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x), 𝔼[Y∣X=x]\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x], and Var⁡(Y)\operatorname{Var}(Y) by total variance.

Method. Check the joint density over its sloping unit-width support. Conditional on X=xX=x, retain the shifted support x<y<x+1x<y<x+1. Then separate the two terms in the law of total variance.

Solution. The proposed density is valid because it is non-negative and

∫01∫xx+11dydx=1. \int_0^1\int_x^{x+1}1\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=1.

For 0<x<10<x<1,

fX(x)=∫xx+11dy=1. f_X(x)=\int_x^{x+1}1\,\mathrm{d}y=1.

The marginal density is zero otherwise. Therefore,

fY∣X(y∣x)=11=1,x<y<x+1, f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) =\frac{1}{1} =1, \qquad x<y<x+1,

for 0<x<10<x<1, and the conditional density is zero outside that conditional support. Hence Y∣X=xY\mid X=x is uniform on (x,x+1)(x,x+1), so

𝔼[Y∣X=x]=x+12,Var⁡(Y∣X=x)=112. \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]=x+\frac12, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)=\frac1{12}.

Because X∼Uniform⁡(0,1)X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1),

Var⁡(X)=112. \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac1{12}.

The first component of total variance is

𝔼[Var⁡(Y∣X)]=112, \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)] =\frac1{12},

and the second is

Var⁡(𝔼[Y∣X])=Var⁡(X+1/2)=Var⁡(X)=112. \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]) =\operatorname{Var}(X+1/2) =\operatorname{Var}(X) =\frac1{12}.

Therefore,

Var⁡(Y)=112+112=16. \operatorname{Var}(Y) =\frac1{12}+\frac1{12} =\frac16.

Interpretation. Total variance adds expected variation within conditional groups to variation between their conditional means. The support must be carried through every density and conditional calculation.
