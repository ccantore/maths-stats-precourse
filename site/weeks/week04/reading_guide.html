Week 4 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcome

By the end of this week, you should be able to calculate partial derivatives, column gradients, Jacobians and Hessians, and apply the multivariable chain rule with dimensionally consistent notation (M6).

Week 4 introduces no new Statistics outcome. Statistics work this week is deliberate consolidation of distributions and moments from S3 and S4. It prepares you to retain the Statistics strand while studying multivariable calculus and before the cumulative Checkpoint A.

Prerequisites and retrieval

You will use one-variable differentiation (M3) and matrix dimensions and multiplication (M4) throughout the new material. The final two questions contain four explicit earlier-outcome retrieval parts:

  • a Taylor approximation (M3);
  • a matrix–vector product with a dimension check (M4);
  • an exponential-distribution probability (S3); and
  • expectation and variance under a linear combination (S4).

Before beginning, retrieve the product rule, chain rule and derivatives of log⁡x\log x and exe^x. Also check that you can determine the dimensions of a matrix product before calculating it.

How to use this route

Required notation translation

After reading, close the source and write the following course conventions from memory:

  • if f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, then ∇f(𝐱)\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) is n×1n\times1;

Core notation and dimensions

Let f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R} be differentiable. Its gradient is the column vector

∇f(𝐱)=[∂f/∂x1⋮∂f/∂xn]∈ℝn. \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^n.

For a small column displacement d𝐱∈ℝn\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n, the total differential is

df=∇f(𝐱)𝖳d𝐱. \mathrm{d}f =\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf T}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The product has dimensions (1×n)(n×1)=1×1(1\times n)(n\times1)=1\times1, as required for a scalar change.

If 𝐅:ℝn→ℝm\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m has components F1,…,FmF_1,\ldots,F_m, its Jacobian is

𝐉𝐅(𝐱)=[∂Fi∂xj]i=1,…,m;j=1,…,n. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j} \right]_{i=1,\ldots,m;\,j=1,\ldots,n}.

It has one row per output and one column per input, so its dimension is m×nm\times n. For a scalar-valued function, the 1×n1\times n Jacobian is the transpose of the n×1n\times1 gradient.

If ff has continuous second partial derivatives near the point of interest, its Hessian is

𝐇f(𝐱)=[∂2f∂xi∂xj]i,j=1,…,n. \mathbf{H}_f(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \frac{\partial^2 f} {\partial x_i\partial x_j} \right]_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}.

Under that continuity condition, the mixed partial derivatives agree and the Hessian is symmetric.

Chain-rule forms

For a differentiable path 𝐱:ℝ→ℝn\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^n and differentiable scalar function f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R},

ddtf(𝐱(t))=∇f(𝐱(t))𝖳𝐱′(t). \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(\mathbf{x}(t)) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}(t))^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}'(t).

For differentiable maps

𝐠:ℝp→ℝn,𝐅:ℝn→ℝm, \mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^n, \qquad \mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m,

the composition 𝐇:=𝐅∘𝐠\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{F}\circ\mathbf{g} maps ℝp\mathbb{R}^p to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m, and

𝐉𝐇(𝐮)=𝐉𝐅(𝐠(𝐮))𝐉𝐠(𝐮). \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf H}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})) \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf{u}).

The dimensions are

(m×n)(n×p)=m×p. (m\times n)(n\times p)=m\times p.

When the outer function is scalar-valued, the equivalent column-gradient form is

∇(f∘𝐠)(𝐮)=𝐉𝐠(𝐮)𝖳∇f(𝐠(𝐮)). \nabla(f\circ\mathbf{g})(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf{u})^{\mathsf T} \nabla f(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})).

Example 1 — a gradient and Hessian (M6)

Let

f(x,y)=x2y+log⁡y,(x,y)∈ℝ×(0,∞). f(x,y)=x^2y+\log y, \qquad (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty).

Calculate the gradient and Hessian at (1,1)(1,1).

Method. Differentiate with respect to one variable while holding the other fixed. State the domain because the logarithm and its derivatives require y>0y>0.

Solution. The first partial derivatives are

fx(x,y)=2xy,fy(x,y)=x2+1y. f_x(x,y)=2xy, \qquad f_y(x,y)=x^2+\frac{1}{y}.

Thus the column gradient is

∇f(x,y)=[2xyx2+1/y],∇f(1,1)=[22]. \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} 2xy\\ x^2+1/y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \nabla f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\2 \end{bmatrix}.

Differentiate the first partial derivatives once more:

fxx=2y,fxy=2x,fyx=2x,fyy=−1y2. f_{xx}=2y, \qquad f_{xy}=2x, \qquad f_{yx}=2x, \qquad f_{yy}=-\frac{1}{y^2}.

Therefore,

𝐇f(x,y)=[2y2x2x−1/y2],𝐇f(1,1)=[222−1]. \mathbf{H}_f(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} 2y&2x\\ 2x&-1/y^2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}_f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2&2\\ 2&-1 \end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation. The gradient is 2×12\times1 because there are two inputs and one output. The Hessian is 2×22\times2. The equal off-diagonal entries agree with the mixed-partial symmetry expected from the continuous second derivatives on the stated domain.

Example 2 — the Jacobian of a vector-valued map (M6)

Define

𝐅:ℝ3→ℝ2,𝐅(x,y,z)=[x2y+zx+yz2]. \mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} x^2y+z\\ x+yz^2 \end{bmatrix}.

Calculate the Jacobian at (1,2,−1)(1,2,-1).

Method. Put the derivatives of one output component in each row and the derivatives with respect to one input in each column.

Solution. The component derivatives are

∇F1(x,y,z)=[2xyx21],∇F2(x,y,z)=[1z22yz]. \begin{aligned} \nabla F_1(x,y,z) &= \begin{bmatrix} 2xy\\x^2\\1 \end{bmatrix}, & \nabla F_2(x,y,z) &= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\z^2\\2yz \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}

The gradients are columns, but the Jacobian uses their transposes as rows:

𝐉𝐅(x,y,z)=[2xyx211z22yz]. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} 2xy&x^2&1\\ 1&z^2&2yz \end{bmatrix}.

At the stated point,

𝐉𝐅(1,2,−1)=[41111−4]. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(1,2,-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 4&1&1\\ 1&1&-4 \end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation. The map has three inputs and two outputs, so its Jacobian must be 2×32\times3. A 3×23\times2 array containing the same numbers would have the wrong orientation under the course convention.

Example 3 — a scalar path through two variables (M6)

Let

f(x,y)=x2ey,𝐠(t)=[1+tt2]. f(x,y)=x^2e^y, \qquad \mathbf{g}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1+t\\ t^2 \end{bmatrix}.

Calculate df(𝐠(t))/dt\mathrm{d}f(\mathbf{g}(t))/\mathrm{d}t at t=0t=0 by the multivariable chain rule and verify it by direct substitution.

Method. Multiply the transposed column gradient by the derivative of the column path. Then differentiate the explicit one-variable composition as a check.

Solution. The required derivatives are

∇f(x,y)=[2xeyx2ey],𝐠′(t)=[12t]. \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} 2xe^y\\ x^2e^y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{g}'(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2t \end{bmatrix}.

At t=0t=0, 𝐠(0)=[1,0]𝖳\mathbf{g}(0)=[1,0]^{\mathsf T}, so

ddtf(𝐠(t))|t=0=[21][10]=2. \left. \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(\mathbf{g}(t)) \right|_{t=0} = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix} =2.

For verification, direct substitution gives

h(t):=f(𝐠(t))=(1+t)2et2. h(t):=f(\mathbf{g}(t))=(1+t)^2e^{t^2}.

Hence

h′(t)=2(1+t)et2+(1+t)2(2t)et2, h'(t) =2(1+t)e^{t^2} +(1+t)^2(2t)e^{t^2},

and h′(0)=2h'(0)=2, as before.

Interpretation. Both coordinates may depend on tt, even though the second coordinate happens to have zero derivative at the evaluation point. The dimensional form is (1×2)(2×1)=1×1(1\times2)(2\times1)=1\times1.

Example 4 — composition of vector-valued maps (M6)

Let

𝐠:ℝ2→ℝ3,𝐠(u,v)=[u2+vu−vuv], \mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3, \qquad \mathbf{g}(u,v) = \begin{bmatrix} u^2+v\\ u-v\\ uv \end{bmatrix},

and

𝐅:ℝ3→ℝ2,𝐅(x,y,z)=[x+2y−zxy+z2]. \mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} x+2y-z\\ xy+z^2 \end{bmatrix}.

Find the Jacobian of 𝐇:=𝐅∘𝐠\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{F}\circ\mathbf{g} at (1,−1)(1,-1).

Method. Evaluate the outer Jacobian at the intermediate point 𝐠(1,−1)\mathbf{g}(1,-1), then multiply it by the inner Jacobian in chain-rule order.

Solution. First,

𝐠(1,−1)=[02−1]. \mathbf{g}(1,-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\2\\-1 \end{bmatrix}.

The two Jacobians are

𝐉𝐠(u,v)=[2u11−1vu] \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf g}(u,v) = \begin{bmatrix} 2u&1\\ 1&-1\\ v&u \end{bmatrix}

and

𝐉𝐅(x,y,z)=[12−1yx2z]. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2&-1\\ y&x&2z \end{bmatrix}.

At the relevant points,

𝐉𝐠(1,−1)=[211−1−11],𝐉𝐅(0,2,−1)=[12−120−2]. \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf g}(1,-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 1&-1\\ -1&1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(0,2,-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2&-1\\ 2&0&-2 \end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

𝐉𝐇(1,−1)=𝐉𝐅(0,2,−1)𝐉𝐠(1,−1)=[12−120−2][211−1−11]=[5−260]. \begin{aligned} \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf H}(1,-1) &= \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf F}(0,2,-1) \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf g}(1,-1)\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1&2&-1\\ 2&0&-2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 1&-1\\ -1&1 \end{bmatrix}\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 5&-2\\ 6&0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}

Interpretation. The dimensions are (2×3)(3×2)=2×2(2\times3)(3\times2)=2\times2, matching a map from two inputs to two outputs. Reversing the factors gives a 3×33\times3 matrix, not the Jacobian of this composition.

Example 5 — a first-order change in an economic quantity (M6)

Let qq denote a dimensionless normalised output index and ww an input-price scale. Consider the differentiable cost index

C(q,w)=w(q+q2),q>0,w>0. C(q,w)=w(q+q^2), \qquad q>0,\quad w>0.

At (q,w)=(2,3)(q,w)=(2,3), approximate the change in CC when dq=1/10\mathrm{d}q=1/10 and dw=−1/5\mathrm{d}w=-1/5.

Method. Calculate the column gradient and form the total differential. Check that the displaced point remains in the economically relevant positive domain.

Solution. The partial derivatives are

Cq(q,w)=w(1+2q),Cw(q,w)=q+q2. C_q(q,w)=w(1+2q), \qquad C_w(q,w)=q+q^2.

Thus,

∇C(2,3)=[156]. \nabla C(2,3) = \begin{bmatrix} 15\\ 6 \end{bmatrix}.

The displaced point is (2+1/10,3−1/5)=(21/10,14/5)(2+1/10,3-1/5)=(21/10,14/5), whose coordinates are both positive. The first-order change is

dC=∇C(2,3)𝖳[1/10−1/5]=15(110)+6(−15)=32−65=310. \begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}C &= \nabla C(2,3)^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix} 1/10\\ -1/5 \end{bmatrix}\\ &= 15\left(\frac1{10}\right) +6\left(-\frac15\right) =\frac32-\frac65 =\frac3{10}. \end{aligned}

Therefore the local approximation predicts an increase of 3/103/10 unit in the index.

Interpretation. Cq(2,3)=15C_q(2,3)=15 is the local rate with respect to quantity while the input price is held fixed; Cw(2,3)=6C_w(2,3)=6 has the analogous holding-quantity-fixed interpretation. The differential combines both changes. It is a first-order approximation for a finite displacement, not an exact equality.

Example 6 — Statistics consolidation (S3, S4)

Let

X∼Exponential⁡(2), X\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(2),

where the parameter 22 is the rate and the support is x≥0x\ge0. Define Z=3−2XZ=3-2X. Find ℙ(X>1)\mathbb{P}(X>1), 𝔼[Z]\mathbb{E}[Z] and Var⁡(Z)\operatorname{Var}(Z).

Method. Retrieve the exponential survival probability and its mean and variance under the rate parameterisation. Then apply the linear transformation rules.

Solution. For an exponential random variable with rate λ=2\lambda=2,

ℙ(X>x)=e−2x,x≥0, \mathbb{P}(X>x)=e^{-2x}, \qquad x\ge0,

so

ℙ(X>1)=e−2. \mathbb{P}(X>1)=e^{-2}.

Also,

𝔼[X]=12,Var⁡(X)=122=14. \mathbb{E}[X]=\frac12, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{2^2}=\frac14.

Therefore,

𝔼[Z]=3−2𝔼[X]=2 \mathbb{E}[Z] =3-2\mathbb{E}[X] =2

and

Var⁡(Z)=(−2)2Var⁡(X)=4(14)=1. \operatorname{Var}(Z) =(-2)^2\operatorname{Var}(X) =4\left(\frac14\right) =1.

Interpretation. The location shift changes the expectation but not the variance. The multiplier is squared in the variance. This is earlier-outcome consolidation, not a new Week 4 Statistics topic.
