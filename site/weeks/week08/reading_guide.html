Week 8 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • construct and interpret elementary confidence intervals for a population mean or proportion under stated normal, large-sample or Student’s t conditions (S8); and
  • formulate null and alternative hypotheses, calculate and interpret test statistics and p-values, distinguish one- and two-sided tests, and analyse Type I error, Type II error, power and test–confidence-interval links (S9).

Prerequisites and retrieval

Retrieve sampling distributions, standard errors, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem (S6). Also retrieve the distinctions among a parameter, an estimand, an estimator and an observed estimate, together with bias, variance, mean squared error and consistency (S7).

The sheet integrates S7 retrieval in W08-Q01-PA. Its final exercise block then contains three further earlier-outcome retrieval parts: W08-Q10-PA (S6), W08-Q10-PB (S7) and W08-Q11-PA (M10, M11). These prepare you for the cumulative Statistics and Maths mock papers without adding a new Week 8 mathematics outcome.

For the newly taught outcomes, later sheet items retrieve methods after their initial teaching and practice: W08-Q06-PB and W08-Q09-PA revisit S8, while W08-Q09-PA revisits S9. The closed-note self-test then provides two further retrieval parts for each of S8 and S9.

End-of-course check

Before finishing, make sure that you can state why each interval or test is valid, not only substitute numbers into a formula. In particular, do not describe a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and do not describe a realised confidence interval as assigning probability to a fixed parameter. Keep the error log for later targeted revision.

Example 1 — a confidence interval for a mean (S8)

Suppose

X1,…,X25∼iid𝒩(μ,16), X_1,\ldots,X_{25}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu,16),

where the population variance 1616 is known. The observed sample mean is x‾=42\bar x=42. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population mean μ\mu. Use z0.975=1.96z_{0.975}=1.96, where ℙ(Z≤z0.975)=0.975\mathbb{P}(Z\le z_{0.975})=0.975 for Z∼𝒩(0,1)Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Method. Identify the fixed parameter, random estimator and observed estimate. Use the exact normal sampling distribution because the population is normal and its variance is known.

Solution. The parameter and estimand are both the population mean μ\mu. The estimator is the random variable X‾\bar X, and its observed estimate is x‾=42\bar x=42. The standard error is

σn=45=0.8. \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} =\frac{4}{5} =0.8.

The random 95% interval is

[X‾−1.9645,X‾+1.9645]. \left[ \bar X-1.96\frac{4}{5}, \bar X+1.96\frac{4}{5} \right].

After observing the data, the realised interval is

[42−1.568,42+1.568]=[40.432,43.568]. [42-1.568,\ 42+1.568]=[40.432,\ 43.568].

Interpretation. In repeated independent samples, 95% of intervals constructed by this method cover the fixed parameter μ\mu. After the sample is observed, this frequentist interval is fixed; it does not assign a 95% probability to the proposition that μ\mu lies between these realised endpoints.

Example 2 — choosing Student’s t (S8)

Suppose X1,…,X9X_1,\ldots,X_9 are independent observations from a normal population with unknown mean μ\mu and unknown variance σ2>0\sigma^2>0. The observed sample mean and sample standard deviation are x‾=12\bar x=12 and s=3s=3. Construct a 95% confidence interval for μ\mu. Use t0.975,8=2.306t_{0.975,8}=2.306.

Method. Because the population is normal, the variance is unknown and it is estimated by S2S^2, use the Student’s t pivot with n−1=8n-1=8 degrees of freedom.

Solution. Under the stated assumptions,

T=X‾−μS/n∼t8. T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_8.

The standard-error estimate is s/n=3/3=1s/\sqrt n=3/3=1. Therefore the realised 95% interval is

[12−2.306,12+2.306]=[9.694,14.306]. \left[ 12-2.306,\ 12+2.306 \right] =[9.694,\ 14.306].

Interpretation. Replacing the unknown σ\sigma by SS changes the reference distribution from standard normal to Student’s t. A small sample does not by itself justify the t method; the normal-population condition is material here.

Example 3 — a one-sided p-value (S9)

Suppose

X1,…,X36∼iid𝒩(μ,36), X_1,\ldots,X_{36}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu,36),

with known variance. An observed sample has x‾=102\bar x=102. Test

H0:μ=100againstH1:μ>100 H_0:\mu=100 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu>100

at significance level α=0.05\alpha=0.05. Use ℙ(Z≥2)=0.0228\mathbb{P}(Z\ge2)=0.0228 and z0.95=1.645z_{0.95}=1.645.

Method. Standardise under the null value and use the upper tail selected by the alternative.

Solution. Under H0H_0,

Z=X‾−1006/36∼𝒩(0,1). Z=\frac{\bar X-100}{6/\sqrt{36}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The observed statistic is

zobs=102−1001=2. z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{102-100}{1}=2.

The p-value is the null probability of a statistic at least as large as the observed value:

ℙH0(Z≥2)=0.0228. \mathbb{P}_{H_0}(Z\ge2)=0.0228.

Because 0.0228<0.050.0228<0.05, reject H0H_0. Equivalently, 2>z0.95=1.6452>z_{0.95}=1.645.

Interpretation. The result is evidence against the null in the stated upper-tail direction. The p-value is not the probability that H0H_0 is true and does not measure the size or practical importance of the difference.

Example 4 — Type II error and power (S9)

Suppose a test of

H0:μ=10againstH1:μ>10 H_0:\mu=10 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu>10

uses one observation X∼𝒩(μ,4)X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,4) and rejects when X>13X>13. Find the Type I error probability. Then find the Type II error probability and power at the specific alternative μ=14\mu=14. Use Φ(1.5)=0.9332\Phi(1.5)=0.9332 and Φ(−0.5)=0.3085\Phi(-0.5)=0.3085, where Φ(z)=ℙ(Z≤z)\Phi(z)=\mathbb{P}(Z\le z).

Method. Evaluate the same fixed rejection rule under two different parameter values. Type I error is rejection under the null; Type II error is non-rejection at the specified alternative.

Solution. At the null value,

α=ℙμ=10(X>13)=ℙ(Z>13−102)=1−Φ(1.5)=0.0668. \alpha =\mathbb{P}_{\mu=10}(X>13) =\mathbb{P}\left(Z>\frac{13-10}{2}\right) =1-\Phi(1.5) =0.0668.

At μ=14\mu=14, the Type II error probability is

β(14)=ℙμ=14(X≤13)=ℙ(Z≤13−142)=Φ(−0.5)=0.3085. \beta(14) =\mathbb{P}_{\mu=14}(X\le13) =\mathbb{P}\left(Z\le\frac{13-14}{2}\right) =\Phi(-0.5) =0.3085.

The power at μ=14\mu=14 is

1−β(14)=0.6915. 1-\beta(14)=0.6915.

Interpretation. The Type II error probability and power must name an alternative parameter value. They are not single properties of a composite alternative without such a value or a power function.
