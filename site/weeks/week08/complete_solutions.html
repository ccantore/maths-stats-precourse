Question 1 — inferential objects and confidence

W08-Q01-PA (S7)

Method and conditions. Separate the fixed population target from the sample function and then from its realised numerical value.

The population parameter is pp, the proportion in the population. The estimand is also pp: it is the population quantity the analysis targets. The estimator is

p̂=X120, \widehat p=\frac{X}{120},

which is a random variable because the count XX varies across possible random samples. After observing x=84x=84, the estimate is

p̂(𝐱)=84120=0.7. \widehat p(\mathbf{x})=\frac{84}{120}=0.7.

This realised number is fixed for the observed data.

Final result. Parameter and estimand: pp; estimator: p̂=X/120\widehat p=X/120; estimate: p̂(𝐱)=0.7\widehat p(\mathbf{x})=0.7.

Marks (5). Parameter, 1; estimand, 1; estimator, 1; numerical estimate, 1; random-before/fixed-after explanation, 1.

Common mistakes. Calling 0.70.7 the estimator; treating the fixed population parameter as random under the frequentist model; failing to distinguish the random count XX from the observed count xx.

W08-Q01-PB (S8)

Method and conditions. Interpret confidence as long-run coverage of a random interval procedure under repeated samples satisfying the method’s conditions.

Before sampling, the endpoints L(𝐗)L(\mathbf{X}) and U(𝐗)U(\mathbf{X}) are random because they are functions of the random sample. A 95% method is calibrated so that

ℙp(L(𝐗)≤p≤U(𝐗))=0.95 \mathbb{P}_p\!\left( L(\mathbf{X})\le p\le U(\mathbf{X}) \right)=0.95

exactly or approximately, according to the method and its conditions.

After the data are observed, the endpoints are realised numbers and the population parameter pp is fixed. In the frequentist interpretation, the realised interval either covers pp or it does not. The construction does not assign a 95% probability to that fixed proposition.

Final result and interpretation. Statement 1 is not the frequentist interpretation. Statement 2 is valid: approximately 95% of intervals from repeated use of the valid method cover the fixed pp.

Marks (5). First statement assessed correctly, 1; repeated-sampling coverage stated, 2; random endpoints identified, 1; fixed realised interval/parameter distinction, 1.

Common mistakes. Assigning posterior probability to a fixed parameter; saying 95% of population observations lie inside the interval; omitting the conditions under which the method has its stated coverage.

Question 9 — test–interval duality and non-rejection

W08-Q09-PA (S8, S9)

Method and conditions. For a two-sided level-0.05 z test and the 95% z interval obtained from the same pivot and standard error, reject a candidate null value exactly when it lies outside the realised interval.

Method and conditions. The independent sample comes from a normal population and the variance is unknown, so use Student’s t with n−1=8n-1=8 degrees of freedom.

The estimated standard error is

sn=33=1. \frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\frac{3}{3}=1.

Thus the realised 95% interval is

[15−2.306,15+2.306]=[12.694,17.306]. [15-2.306,\ 15+2.306]=[12.694,\ 17.306].

In repeated iid samples from the stated normal model, 95% of intervals constructed by this method cover μ\mu.

Marks (5). Method, condition and degrees of freedom, 2; standard error, 1; endpoints, 1; valid interpretation, 1.

Common mistakes. Using a z critical value with unknown variance; using 9 degrees of freedom; assigning 95% probability to the fixed μ\mu.

Solution to W08-ST02-PA (S8)

Method and conditions. The estimate is p̂=40/100=0.4\widehat p=40/100=0.4. There are 40 successes and 60 failures, so the large-sample normal approximation is reasonable under independent random sampling.

The realised interval is

[0.4−1.96(0.0490),0.4+1.96(0.0490)]≈[0.304,0.496]. \begin{aligned} \left[ 0.4-1.96(0.0490), 0.4+1.96(0.0490) \right] &\approx[0.304,\ 0.496]. \end{aligned}

In repeated independent samples, approximately 95% of intervals constructed by this large-sample method cover the fixed population proportion pp. For the observed data, the endpoints are fixed; the interval does not assign a 95% probability to pp.

Marks (5). Estimate, 1; two count checks and independence condition, 1; standard error or valid interval setup, 1; endpoints, 1; repeated-sampling interpretation, 1.

Common mistakes. Checking only the success count; using 40 as the sample size; using p=0.5p=0.5 in the observed interval; interpreting the interval as a range containing 95% of individual responses.

Solution to W08-ST05-PA (S9)

Method and conditions. The observations are independent and normal, the population variance is unknown, and the alternative selects the upper tail. Under H0H_0,

T=X‾−20S/16∼t15. T=\frac{\bar X-20}{S/\sqrt{16}}\sim t_{15}.

The observed statistic is

tobs=21.5−204/4=1.5. t_{\mathrm{obs}} =\frac{21.5-20}{4/4} =1.5.

The upper-tail p-value is 0.0770.077. Because 0.077>0.050.077>0.05, do not reject H0H_0.

The p-value is the null probability of a t statistic at least as large as 1.5 in the stated upper-tail direction. It is not the probability that H0H_0 is true, and non-rejection does not prove that μ=20\mu=20.

Marks (5). Student’s t method, upper-tail direction and degrees of freedom, 1; standard error and statistic, 1; p-value, 1; decision, 1; interpretation, 1.

Solution to W08-ST04-PA (S9)

Method and conditions. The sample mean has distribution

X‾∼𝒩(μ,44)=𝒩(μ,1). \bar X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\frac{4}{4}\right) =\mathcal{N}(\mu,1).

α=ℙμ=0(X‾>1.645)=ℙ(Z>1.645)=0.05. \alpha =\mathbb{P}_{\mu=0}(\bar X>1.645) =\mathbb{P}(Z>1.645) =0.05.

At μ=2\mu=2, a Type II error is non-rejection:

β(2)=ℙμ=2(X‾≤1.645)=Φ(1.645−2)=Φ(−0.355)=0.3613. \begin{aligned} \beta(2) &=\mathbb{P}_{\mu=2}(\bar X\le1.645)\\ &=\Phi(1.645-2)\\ &=\Phi(-0.355)\\ &=0.3613. \end{aligned}

The power at μ=2\mu=2 is

1−β(2)=0.6387. 1-\beta(2)=0.6387.

Marks (5). Sampling distribution, 1; Type I probability, 1; Type II event and standardisation, 1; Type II value, 1; power, 1.
