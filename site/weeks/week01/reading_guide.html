Week 1 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • interpret and use set, function, quantifier, implication and equivalence notation, and complete short arguments by direct reasoning, contradiction or induction (M1);
  • manipulate powers, logarithms, functions, indexed sums, products and finite or convergent geometric series (M2);
  • calculate and interpret derivatives and elementary integrals, construct first- and second-order Taylor approximations, and solve elementary one-variable optimisation problems (M3); and
  • represent probability questions as events and apply set operations, probability rules and elementary counting (S1).

Prerequisites and retrieval

Retrieve fraction arithmetic, exponent rules, elementary equation solving and the meaning of a function before beginning the readings. No earlier course outcome is a formal prerequisite for Week 1. Write down any rule that you cannot reproduce reliably and revisit it during the first study block.

Student materials

Use the Week 1 reading guide for exact written sections and skip instructions. Then study and reproduce the original worked examples. Complete the exercise sheet, consulting progressive hints only after a serious attempt. Use the complete solutions to mark your work after the attempt is finished. End with the 25-minute self-test and update your error log from its solutions.

The external readings remain on their authors’ or institutions’ sites. Open only the assigned sections. No video, applet, code or specialist software is required.

Before beginning the Week 1 readings, use the required notation and conventions bridge note. It is point-of-need support within the existing reading and worked-example allocation, not additional workload.

Suggested study sequence

  1. Retrieve fraction and exponent rules and write the four outcome IDs at the top of your notes.
  2. Complete the readings in the listed order. Close each source and reproduce at least one important definition, argument or worked step for each outcome.
  3. Study the four institutional worked examples. Cover each solution, follow the method and reproduce the work from a blank page.
  4. Attempt all questions without hints or solutions. A basic scientific calculator is permitted only for the logarithmic estimate in W01-Q03-PB; it is not required elsewhere.
  5. If you are stuck, use the hints in order and stop as soon as you have a useful next step.
  6. Mark from the full solutions. Record the first cause of each error under M1, M2, M3 or S1.
  7. Take the 25-minute self-test without notes, mark it and add any new cause of error to the same outcome-labelled log.
  8. Reattempt each incorrect or incomplete part from a blank page two or three days later.

Worked examples

These original institutional examples support M1, M2, M3 and S1. For each example, cover the Solution, follow the Method, and reproduce the work from a blank page before reading the Interpretation. Allow about 65 minutes for study and reproduction.

Example 1 — a direct argument (M1)

Show that the square of every even integer is even.

Method. Begin with the definition of an even integer and use direct reasoning. The conclusion must have the same definition.

Solution. Let n∈ℤn\in\mathbb{Z} be even. Then there is an integer kk such that n=2kn=2k. Therefore,

n2=(2k)2=4k2=2(2k2). n^2=(2k)^2=4k^2=2(2k^2).

Because 2k2∈ℤ2k^2\in\mathbb{Z}, the number n2n^2 is twice an integer and is therefore even.

Interpretation. The argument does not rely on a few numerical examples. It begins with an arbitrary even integer, so it establishes the implication for every even integer.

Example 2 — a declining geometric stream (M2)

A programme pays 8080 units at t=0t=0, and each later payment is one half of the preceding payment. Find the infinite total. Then find the smallest final included period NN for which the cumulative total over t=0,…,Nt=0,\ldots,N is at least 150150.

Method. Identify the first term and common ratio, apply both geometric sum formulae, and verify the integer boundary.

Solution. The common ratio is r=1/2r=1/2, so |r|<1|r|<1 and the infinite series converges. Its total is

∑t=0∞80(12)t=801−1/2=160. \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}80\left(\frac12\right)^t =\frac{80}{1-1/2} =160.

The finite total is

SN=801−(1/2)N+11−1/2=160(1−2−(N+1)). S_N =80\frac{1-(1/2)^{N+1}}{1-1/2} =160\left(1-2^{-(N+1)}\right).

The threshold condition is

160(1−2−(N+1))≥150,2−(N+1)≤116=2−4. \begin{aligned} 160\left(1-2^{-(N+1)}\right)&\ge150,\\ 2^{-(N+1)}&\le\frac{1}{16}=2^{-4}. \end{aligned}

Because 2x2^x is strictly increasing, −(N+1)≤−4-(N+1)\le-4, so N≥3N\ge3. The boundary check gives

S2=140<150,S3=150. S_2=140<150, \qquad S_3=150.

Thus the smallest final period is N=3N=3.

Interpretation. The index starts at zero, so N=3N=3 includes four payments. The boundary check prevents confusing the final index with the number of included periods.

Example 3 — a global maximum on an interval (M3)

Let

p(q)=12q−q2,q∈[0,10]. p(q)=12q-q^2, \qquad q\in[0,10].

Find the global maximum and the maximising value of qq.

Method. Find every interior stationary point and compare its value with both endpoints. Use curvature as an additional sufficiency check.

Solution. The first two derivatives are

p′(q)=12−2q,p″(q)=−2. p'(q)=12-2q, \qquad p''(q)=-2.

The unique stationary point is q=6q=6, which lies inside the feasible interval. The candidate values are

p(0)=0,p(6)=36,p(10)=20. p(0)=0, \qquad p(6)=36, \qquad p(10)=20.

Therefore the global maximum is 3636, attained at q=6q=6. The strict concavity p″(q)<0p''(q)<0 confirms that there is at most one maximiser.

Interpretation. A first-order condition identifies a candidate. The endpoint comparison is what establishes the global conclusion on the stated closed interval.

Example 4 — counting with a complement (S1)

Two representatives are selected uniformly from five people, and order does not matter. Two of the five are designated priority representatives. Find the probability that at least one priority representative is selected.

Method. Count all unordered pairs and subtract the pairs that contain no priority representative. Reconcile this with a direct count.

Solution. There are

(52)=10 \binom{5}{2}=10

equally likely pairs. A pair with no priority representative must choose both people from the other three, giving (32)=3\binom{3}{2}=3 pairs. Hence

ℙ(at least one priority representative)=1−(32)(52)=710. \mathbb{P}(\text{at least one priority representative}) =1-\frac{\binom{3}{2}}{\binom{5}{2}} =\frac{7}{10}.

Directly, there are 2⋅3=62\cdot3=6 pairs with exactly one priority representative and one pair with both, for the same favourable total of seven.

Interpretation. The events “exactly one” and “both” are disjoint, so their counts may be added without double-counting.
