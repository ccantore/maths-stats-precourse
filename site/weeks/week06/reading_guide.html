Week 6 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • solve unconstrained multivariable optimisation problems and distinguish candidates, local optima and global optima using necessary and sufficient conditions (M9); and
  • work with independent and identically distributed samples, sample means and sample variances, use elementary sampling distributions, and distinguish and apply the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem (S6).

Prerequisites and retrieval

For optimisation, retrieve column gradients (M6), implicit differentiation (M7) and definiteness, convexity and concavity (M8). For sampling, retrieve algebra (M2), standard distributions (S3), expectation and variance (S4), and conditional moments (S5). Questions 9–11 are a cumulative block on M6–M8 and S3–S5. Complete those three parts without consulting earlier notes.

Student materials

Use the reading guide for the exact written sections, locators and skip instructions. Then study and reproduce the original worked examples. Attempt all parts of the exercise sheet before consulting the progressive hints or complete solutions. Finish with the 25-minute self-test, mark it, and update your error log by outcome ID.

The external readings remain on their authors’ sites. No external exercise is reproduced here. No video, applet, code or specialist software is required.

Suggested study sequence

  1. Review column gradients, Hessian definiteness, normal means and variances.
  2. Complete the exact reading selections and their closed-source summaries.
  3. Cover each worked solution, reproduce the example from a blank page, and explain which conditions make the conclusion valid.
  4. Attempt all 16 scorable exercise parts. Use hints only after a serious attempt and stop as soon as a hint supplies a useful next step.
  5. Mark from the full solutions. Record the first cause of each error under M9, S6, or the relevant retrieval outcome.
  6. Take the self-test without notes. Use the final five minutes of the weekly review block to record errors and schedule a blank-page reattempt two or three days later.

Optimisation conditions

Let f:ℝn→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}. A point is a candidate when it has survived the relevant necessary-condition and feasibility checks; it is not thereby an optimum. If ff is differentiable and 𝐱*\mathbf{x}^\ast is an interior local optimum, then the first-order necessary condition is

∇f(𝐱*)=𝟎. \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\ast)=\mathbf{0}.

If ff is twice continuously differentiable near a stationary point, a positive definite Hessian is sufficient for a strict local minimum and a negative definite Hessian is sufficient for a strict local maximum. An indefinite Hessian identifies a saddle point. A semidefinite or zero Hessian does not generally settle the classification.

Local and global conclusions are different. Strict concavity on a convex domain makes any stationary point the unique global maximiser, provided the point exists in the domain. More generally, a continuous objective on a non-empty closed and bounded feasible set attains both a maximum and a minimum. This operational existence statement is enough here; no topological proof is required.

Example 1 — classifying stationary points (M9)

Let

f(x,y)=(x2−1)2+y2. f(x,y)=(x^2-1)^2+y^2.

Find and classify every stationary point.

Method. Set the column gradient equal to zero. Evaluate the Hessian at each candidate and classify it by definiteness.

Solution. The column gradient and Hessian are

∇f(x,y)=[4x(x2−1)2y],𝐇f(x,y)=[12x2−4002]. \nabla f(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} 4x(x^2-1)\\ 2y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}_f(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} 12x^2-4&0\\ 0&2 \end{bmatrix}.

The stationary equations give x∈{−1,0,1}x\in\{-1,0,1\} and y=0y=0. At (−1,0)(-1,0) and (1,0)(1,0), the Hessian is diag⁡(8,2)\operatorname{diag}(8,2), which is positive definite, so both points are strict local minima. At (0,0)(0,0), the Hessian is diag⁡(−4,2)\operatorname{diag}(-4,2), which is indefinite, so the origin is a saddle point.

Interpretation. The first-order condition produces three candidates, and the Hessian distinguishes their local behaviour. The expression defining ff is non-negative and equals zero only at (−1,0)(-1,0) and (1,0)(1,0), so those two points are also global minimisers. That global conclusion comes from the objective on the whole domain, not from evaluating the Hessian only at the candidates.

Example 2 — upgrading a local result to a global one (M9)

Let

g(x,y)=log⁡x+log⁡y−x−y,(x,y)∈(0,∞)2. g(x,y)=\log x+\log y-x-y, \qquad (x,y)\in(0,\infty)^2.

Find the global maximiser and maximum value.

Method. Solve the stationary equations. Use the Hessian to establish strict concavity on the whole domain, then evaluate the objective.

Solution. We have

∇g(x,y)=[1/x−11/y−1],𝐇g(x,y)=[−1/x200−1/y2]. \nabla g(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} 1/x-1\\ 1/y-1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}_g(x,y)= \begin{bmatrix} -1/x^2&0\\ 0&-1/y^2 \end{bmatrix}.

The unique stationary point is (1,1)(1,1). For every point in the domain and every non-zero vector [a,b]𝖳[a,b]^{\mathsf T},

[ab]𝐇g(x,y)[ab]=−a2x2−b2y2<0. \begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix} \mathbf{H}_g(x,y) \begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix} =-\frac{a^2}{x^2}-\frac{b^2}{y^2}<0.

Thus the Hessian is negative definite everywhere, so gg is strictly concave on the convex domain (0,∞)2(0,\infty)^2. Hence the stationary point is the unique global maximiser. The maximum value is

g(1,1)=−2. g(1,1)=-2.

Interpretation. Negative definiteness at a point gives a strict local maximum. Negative definiteness everywhere on the convex domain supplies the global strict-concavity argument. The domain and the curvature calculation are different from merely classifying a constant quadratic Hessian.

Random samples and sampling distributions

Write

X1,…,Xn∼iidF X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}F

only when the observations are both independent and identically distributed. If their common mean and variance are μ\mu and σ2<∞\sigma^2<\infty, then

𝔼[X‾]=μ,Var⁡(X‾)=σ2n. \mathbb{E}[\bar X]=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.

The sample variance convention is

S2=1n−1∑i=1n(Xi−X‾)2. S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)^2.

For a normal random sample,

X‾∼𝒩(μ,σ2n) \bar X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)

exactly, and

(n−1)S2σ2∼χn−12. \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.

For a non-normal i.i.d. sample with finite, positive variance, the central limit theorem instead gives the large-nn approximation

X‾−μσ/n∼approximately𝒩(0,1). \frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ \overset{\text{approximately}}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}(0,1).

Example 3 — an exact sampling distribution for a count (S6)

Suppose

X1,…,X10∼iidUniform⁡(0,1), X_1,\ldots,X_{10}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1),

and let

K:=∑i=110𝟏{Xi>3/4}. K:=\sum_{i=1}^{10}\mathbf{1}\{X_i>3/4\}.

Find the exact distribution of KK, and calculate ℙ(K=0)\mathbb{P}(K=0).

Method. Identify the distribution of each indicator, then use independence to recognise the distribution of their sum.

Solution. For each observation,

ℙ(Xi>3/4)=14. \mathbb{P}(X_i>3/4)=\frac14.

The indicators are independent Bernoulli⁡(1/4)\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/4) random variables. Hence

K∼Binomial⁡(10,14) K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\left(10,\frac14\right)

exactly, and

ℙ(K=0)=(100)(14)0(34)10=(34)10. \mathbb{P}(K=0) =\binom{10}{0}\left(\frac14\right)^0 \left(\frac34\right)^{10} =\left(\frac34\right)^{10}.

Interpretation. A sampling distribution need not be normal and need not belong to the sample mean. Here the statistic counts observations with a specified property, and its exact law follows from the i.i.d. sampling model.

Example 4 — independence is not identical distribution (S6)

Suppose X1,X2,X3X_1,X_2,X_3 are independent and

X1∼𝒩(2,1),X2∼𝒩(4,4),X3∼𝒩(6,9). X_1\sim\mathcal{N}(2,1),\qquad X_2\sim\mathcal{N}(4,4),\qquad X_3\sim\mathcal{N}(6,9).

For A:=(X1+X2+X3)/3A:=(X_1+X_2+X_3)/3, find the exact distribution of AA. Decide whether these observations form an i.i.d. random sample and whether the course’s i.i.d. LLN or CLT can be invoked from the stated information.

Method. Use independence to combine normal variables, but check independence and identical distribution as separate conditions.

Solution. Independence gives

𝔼[A]=2+4+63=4 \mathbb{E}[A]=\frac{2+4+6}{3}=4

and

Var⁡(A)=1+4+932=149. \operatorname{Var}(A) =\frac{1+4+9}{3^2} =\frac{14}{9}.

Because a linear combination of independent normal variables is normal,

A∼𝒩(4,149) A\sim\mathcal{N}\left(4,\frac{14}{9}\right)

exactly. The observations are independent, but they are not identically distributed: their means and variances differ. Therefore they cannot be written as X1,X2,X3∼iidFX_1,X_2,X_3\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}F, and the course’s i.i.d. LLN or CLT does not follow from these three observations and assumptions.

Interpretation. Independence allows variances to add in the calculation of AA, but it does not make the marginal distributions identical. Check both words in “i.i.d.” before using a random-sample formula or a limiting theorem.
