Week 3 reading guide and worked examples

Weekly overview

Required learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • M5: determine span, linear independence, bases and dimension; compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors; and diagonalise a simple real matrix when diagonalisation is possible;
  • S3: use probability mass functions (PMFs), probability density functions (PDFs) and cumulative distribution functions (CDFs), and work with the required discrete and continuous distributions under the course parameterisations; and
  • S4: calculate and interpret expectations, variances, covariances and correlations, including under linear transformations and by using indicator variables.

Prerequisites and retrieval

You will use matrix operations, determinants, rank and elimination from M4; algebra and logarithms from M2; probability rules from S1; and conditional probability from S2. Question 10 contains three explicit earlier-outcome retrieval parts on M4, S2 and M2.

Before beginning, check that you can multiply a matrix by a column vector, row-reduce a small augmented matrix, solve a quadratic equation and apply the multiplication rule for probabilities.

Suggested study sequence

  1. Retrieve the prerequisite methods and write M5, S3 and S4 at the top of your error log.
  2. Complete the vector-space and eigenstructure reading. Reproduce Worked Examples 1 and 2 from a blank page.
  3. Complete the random-variable and distribution readings. Build one distribution card that states the course parameterisation and support for every required distribution.
  4. Complete the moments reading. Reproduce Worked Examples 3–5, checking the distinction between a PMF, PDF and CDF and between covariance and correlation.

  6. Use hints one at a time only when a written attempt has reached a specific impasse.
  7. Mark the sheet, record the first cause of each error and take the self-test without notes.
  8. Reattempt every incorrect or incomplete part from a blank page two or three days later.

End-of-week check

Before moving on, make sure that you can state why a proposed spanning set is or is not a basis, when a matrix is diagonalisable over the real numbers, and why a density value is not itself a probability. You should also be able to state the support and parameterisation of each required distribution without consulting the source.

Worked examples

These are original institutional notes and examples for M5, S3 and S4. Their study time is included in the 335-minute registered reading-and-example block. Cover each Solution, follow the Method and reproduce the calculation from a blank page. All vectors are column vectors.

Definitions and conventions

For vectors 𝐯1,…,𝐯k∈ℝn\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k\in\mathbb{R}^n, their span is

span⁡{𝐯1,…,𝐯k}:={∑i=1kai𝐯i:a1,…,ak∈ℝ}. \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k\} := \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i\mathbf{v}_i:a_1,\ldots,a_k\in\mathbb{R} \right\}.

The vectors are linearly independent when

∑i=1kai𝐯i=𝟎⇒a1=⋯=ak=0. \sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_1=\cdots=a_k=0.

A basis of a subspace is a linearly independent spanning set. The dimension is the number of vectors in any basis of that subspace.

For a square real matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A}, a non-zero vector 𝐯\mathbf{v} is an eigenvector associated with λ\lambda when

𝐀𝐯=λ𝐯. \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}.

An n×nn\times n matrix is diagonalisable over ℝ\mathbb{R} when it has nn linearly independent real eigenvectors. If these eigenvectors are the columns of 𝐏\mathbf{P}, then

𝐀=𝐏𝐃𝐏−1, \mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1},

where 𝐃\mathbf{D} contains the corresponding eigenvalues on its diagonal.

For probability, use pX(x)p_X(x) for a PMF, fX(x)f_X(x) for a PDF and FX(x)=ℙ(X≤x)F_X(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\le x) for a CDF. A PMF sums to one over its support. A PDF is non-negative and integrates to one over its support. A density value fX(x)f_X(x) is not a point probability: for a continuous random variable, ℙ(X=x)=0\mathbb{P}(X=x)=0.

Example 1 — span, independence and coordinates (M5)

Let

𝐯1=[100],𝐯2=[110],𝐯3=[111]. \mathbf{v}_1= \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2= \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_3= \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}.

Show that ℬ=(𝐯1,𝐯2,𝐯3)\mathcal{B}=(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3) is a basis of ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3, then find the coordinates of 𝐱=[6,5,3]𝖳\mathbf{x}=[6,5,3]^{\mathsf T} relative to ℬ\mathcal{B}.

Method. Put the vectors into the columns of a matrix. A non-zero determinant establishes independence; three independent vectors in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3 form a basis. Solve the coefficient equation for the coordinates.

Solution. Let

𝐏=[111011001]. \mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1\\ 0&1&1\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}.

Expansion along the first row gives

det⁡(𝐏)=1≠0. \det(\mathbf{P}) =1\ne0.

Thus the columns are linearly independent. Since there are three of them in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3, they also span ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3, so ℬ\mathcal{B} is a basis.

Write

a𝐯1+b𝐯2+c𝐯3=[653]. a\mathbf{v}_1+b\mathbf{v}_2+c\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}6\\5\\3\end{bmatrix}.

The coefficient equations are

a+b+c=6,b+c=5,c=3. a+b+c=6,\qquad b+c=5,\qquad c=3.

The third equation gives c=3c=3; the second then gives b=2b=2, and the first gives a=1a=1. Therefore,

[𝐱]ℬ=[123]. [\mathbf{x}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation. The same coefficient calculation both represents 𝐱\mathbf{x} as a linear combination and gives its coordinates in the ordered basis. Reordering the basis would reorder the coordinate vector.

Example 2 — eigenvalues and diagonalisation (M5)

Let

𝐀=[1221]. \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\ 2&1 \end{bmatrix}.

Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors and diagonalise it over ℝ\mathbb{R}.

Method. Solve the characteristic equation det⁡(𝐀−λ𝐈2)=0\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_2)=0. For each eigenvalue, solve (𝐀−λ𝐈2)𝐯=𝟎(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_2)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}. Put independent eigenvectors in the columns of 𝐏\mathbf{P} in the same order as their eigenvalues in 𝐃\mathbf{D}.

Solution. The characteristic polynomial is

det⁡(𝐀−λ𝐈2)=∣1−λ221−λ∣=(1−λ)2−4=λ2−2λ−3=(λ−3)(λ+1). \begin{aligned} \det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_2) &= \begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda&2\\ 2&1-\lambda \end{vmatrix}\\ &=(1-\lambda)^2-4\\ &=\lambda^2-2\lambda-3\\ &=(\lambda-3)(\lambda+1). \end{aligned}

Thus the eigenvalues are 33 and −1-1.

For λ=3\lambda=3,

(𝐀−3𝐈2)𝐯=[−222−2]𝐯=𝟎, (\mathbf{A}-3\mathbf{I}_2)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}-2&2\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} =\mathbf{0},

so one eigenvector is 𝐯1=[1,1]𝖳\mathbf{v}_1=[1,1]^{\mathsf T}. For λ=−1\lambda=-1,

(𝐀+𝐈2)𝐯=[2222]𝐯=𝟎, (\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I}_2)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} =\mathbf{0},

so one eigenvector is 𝐯2=[1,−1]𝖳\mathbf{v}_2=[1,-1]^{\mathsf T}.

These eigenvectors are independent because

det⁡[111−1]=−2≠0. \det \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-1 \end{bmatrix} =-2\ne0.

Therefore, with

𝐏=[111−1],𝐃=[300−1], \mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 3&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix},

the matrix is diagonalisable and 𝐀=𝐏𝐃𝐏−1\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}. Indeed,

𝐏−1=[1/21/21/2−1/2]. \mathbf{P}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2&1/2\\ 1/2&-1/2 \end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation. The columns of 𝐏\mathbf{P} and the diagonal entries of 𝐃\mathbf{D} must remain paired. Distinct real eigenvalues guarantee independent eigenvectors in this 2×22\times2 case.

Example 3 — from a PMF to a CDF and moments (S3, S4)

Let XX have support {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\} and PMF

pX(x)=c(2x+1),x∈{0,1,2}. p_X(x)=c(2x+1), \qquad x\in\{0,1,2\}.

Find cc, the CDF, the mean and the variance.

Method. Normalise the PMF, accumulate its masses to form the CDF, then calculate 𝔼[X]\mathbb{E}[X] and 𝔼[X2]\mathbb{E}[X^2].

Solution. Normalisation requires

c(1+3+5)=9c=1, c(1+3+5)=9c=1,

so c=1/9c=1/9. The CDF is

FX(x)={0,x<0,1/9,0≤x<1,4/9,1≤x<2,1,x≥2. F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1/9, & 0\le x<1,\\ 4/9, & 1\le x<2,\\ 1, & x\ge2. \end{cases}

The first two moments needed for the variance are

𝔼[X]=019+139+259=139 \mathbb{E}[X] =0\frac19+1\frac39+2\frac59 =\frac{13}{9}

and

𝔼[X2]=0219+1239+2259=239. \mathbb{E}[X^2] =0^2\frac19+1^2\frac39+2^2\frac59 =\frac{23}{9}.

Therefore,

Var⁡(X)=𝔼[X2]−𝔼[X]2=239−(139)2=3881. \operatorname{Var}(X) =\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2 =\frac{23}{9}-\left(\frac{13}{9}\right)^2 =\frac{38}{81}.

Interpretation. A discrete CDF is right-continuous and jumps by the PMF mass at each support point.

Example 4 — a continuous distribution and a linear transformation (S3, S4)

Let T∼Exponential⁡(3)T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(3), where 33 is the rate. Find ℙ(T>2)\mathbb{P}(T>2), then find the mean and variance of Y=2T+4Y=2T+4.

Method. State the support and exponential parameterisation. Integrate the density for the tail, then use the linear-transformation rules for moments.

Solution. The support is t≥0t\ge0, and the density is

fT(t)=3e−3t,t≥0. f_T(t)=3e^{-3t}, \qquad t\ge0.

Thus

ℙ(T>2)=∫2∞3e−3tdt=e−6. \mathbb{P}(T>2) =\int_2^\infty 3e^{-3t}\,\mathrm{d}t =e^{-6}.

Since the rate is 33,

𝔼[T]=13,Var⁡(T)=19. \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac13, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac19.

For Y=2T+4Y=2T+4,

𝔼[Y]=2𝔼[T]+4=143, \mathbb{E}[Y] =2\mathbb{E}[T]+4 =\frac{14}{3},

and

Var⁡(Y)=22Var⁡(T)=49. \operatorname{Var}(Y) =2^2\operatorname{Var}(T) =\frac49.

Interpretation. Adding a constant changes the mean but not the variance. Multiplying by 22 multiplies variance by 222^2.

Example 5 — indicators, covariance and dependence (S4)

Two cards are drawn without replacement from six cards, of which two are red. Let I1I_1 indicate that the first card is red and I2I_2 indicate that the second card is red. Let R=I1+I2R=I_1+I_2, the number of red cards drawn. Find 𝔼[R]\mathbb{E}[R], Var⁡(R)\operatorname{Var}(R) and Corr⁡(I1,I2)\operatorname{Corr}(I_1,I_2).

Method. Each indicator is Bernoulli. Use linearity of expectation and include the covariance term in the variance of the sum.

Solution. By symmetry,

ℙ(I1=1)=ℙ(I2=1)=13. \mathbb{P}(I_1=1)=\mathbb{P}(I_2=1)=\frac13.

Therefore,

𝔼[I1]=𝔼[I2]=13,Var⁡(I1)=Var⁡(I2)=29. \mathbb{E}[I_1]=\mathbb{E}[I_2]=\frac13, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(I_1)=\operatorname{Var}(I_2)=\frac29.

The probability that both cards are red is

ℙ(I1=1,I2=1)=1315=115. \mathbb{P}(I_1=1,I_2=1) =\frac13\frac15 =\frac1{15}.

Hence

Cov⁡(I1,I2)=𝔼[I1I2]−𝔼[I1]𝔼[I2]=115−19=−245. \operatorname{Cov}(I_1,I_2) =\mathbb{E}[I_1I_2]-\mathbb{E}[I_1]\mathbb{E}[I_2] =\frac1{15}-\frac19 =-\frac2{45}.

It follows that

𝔼[R]=13+13=23 \mathbb{E}[R]=\frac13+\frac13=\frac23

and

Var⁡(R)=29+29+2(−245)=1645. \operatorname{Var}(R) =\frac29+\frac29+2\left(-\frac2{45}\right) =\frac{16}{45}.

Finally,

Corr⁡(I1,I2)=−2/45(2/3)(2/3)=−15. \operatorname{Corr}(I_1,I_2) =\frac{-2/45}{(\sqrt{2}/3)(\sqrt{2}/3)} =-\frac15.

Interpretation. The negative covariance reflects sampling without replacement: a red card on the first draw makes a red card less likely on the second draw.
